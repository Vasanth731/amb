Section 3Vectors

  • Scalar → magnitude only (how much)
  • Vector → magnitude and direction (how much and which way)

Vectors are added head to tail; the resultant closes the triangle and \(A^{2} + B^{2} = C^{2}\) for perpendicular components. Subtraction: \(\vec{A} - \vec{B} = \vec{A} + (-\vec{B})\).

\[ A_y = A\sin\theta, \qquad A_x = A\cos\theta \]

A vector times a scalar retains direction (\(\vec{A} \rightarrow 2\vec{A}\)); the cross product \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}\) has magnitude \(|a\,b|\sin\theta\).

Position, velocity, acceleration

  • Position → distance from a reference point (\(1\,\mathrm{m}\))
  • Velocity → change in position per unit time (\(1\,\mathrm{m/s}\))
  • Acceleration → change in velocity per unit time (\(1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\))
  • Distance (scalar) vs. displacement (vector)

Section 4Kinematics

Speed is a scalar, velocity a vector: two runners at the same speed (\(3\,\mathrm{m/s}\)) have the same magnitude but, in different directions, different velocities.

\[ \text{average speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}, \qquad \text{average velocity} = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}}, \qquad \text{average acceleration} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \]

Acceleration is always a vector. At constant acceleration the velocity gradually increases; deceleration is negative acceleration (a long vector backwards). Mechanics splits into kinematics (Galileo) — motion of objects without reference to forces — and dynamics — forces and their influence on the motion of objects.

Position, velocity and acceleration as functions of time

The position, velocity and acceleration of an object in motion can be written as functions of time:

\[ s(t) = 5t^{2} + 2t + 1, \qquad v(t) = 10t + 2, \qquad a(t) = 10 \]

Velocity is the derivative of displacement and acceleration is the derivative of velocity:

\[ \frac{ds}{dt} = v, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^{2}s}{dt^{2}} = a \]

Equations of motion

The four working equations, with the variable each one leaves out:

\[ v_f = v_i + at \quad \text{(missing } d\text{)} \] \[ x_f = x_0 + v_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^{2} \quad \text{(missing } v_f\text{)} \] \[ v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2ad \quad \text{(missing } t\text{)} \] \[ d = \left(\frac{v_f + v_i}{2}\right)t, \qquad \bar{v} = \frac{v_f + v_i}{2} \]

Derivation from acceleration

\[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} \] \[ \int_{v_0}^{v} dv = \int a\,dt \] \[ v - v_0 = at \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad v = v_0 + at \]

Derivation from velocity

\[ v = \frac{ds}{dt} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad v_0 + at = \frac{ds}{dt} \] \[ \int v_0\,dt + \int at\,dt = \int ds \] \[ s = v_0 t + \frac{at^{2}}{2} \]

Derivation using the chain rule

\[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{ds}\frac{ds}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{ds} \] \[ \int a\,ds = \int v\,dv \] \[ as = \frac{v^{2}}{2} - \frac{v_0^{2}}{2} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad v_0^{2} + 2as = v^{2} \]

Vertical velocity

\(a = -9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) on Earth; displacement is the same, \(v_i\) is taken as \(+\)ve, and the same equations apply.

Projectile motion

A curved path due to gravity; horizontal and vertical motion are independent and take the same time.

  • Vertical → time spent in air (\(d_y\)) → \(Y\) direction
  • Horizontal → distance it travels from the cliff (\(v_x\))

The time it spends in air relates only to the \(Y\)-direction behaviour: no matter what the horizontal velocity is, the vertical motion is independent of it. The distance travelled depends on the horizontal velocity, and also on the time it spends in air: \(d = v_x t\). So for \(v_i\) at \(30^{\circ}\), \(v_y\) sets the time in air and \(v_x\) sets how far.

Problem-solving steps

  1. Data: what do I have — \(d\), \(v_i\), \(v_f\), \(a\), \(t\)?
  2. What am I trying to find?
  3. Which equation to use?
  4. Does it make sense (the answer)?

Section 5Forces and Newton's laws

Forces are vectors: contact forces and non-contact forces (field forces, which act at the microscopic level). \(1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s^2}\).

First law

Motion in space is normal motion; motion on earth is a special case.

An object stays at rest or continues to move at a constant velocity unless another force acts on it. The capacity of an object to resist changes in motion is inertia, and inertia \(\propto\) mass. Compare a ping-pong ball with a bowling ball: mass is the quantitative measurement of an object's inertia (at rest or in motion). An object will preserve its state of motion unless acted upon by an external net force.

In terms of momentum,

\[ p = mv, \qquad \frac{dp}{dt} = m\frac{dv}{dt} \]

so if the velocity is unchanging then \(\dfrac{dv}{dt} = 0\) and therefore \(F = 0\) — which is the law of inertia.

Second law — the law of acceleration

\[ F = ma, \qquad a \propto F, \qquad a \propto \frac{1}{m}, \qquad a = \frac{F}{m} \]

Third law

For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Taking two bodies together,

\[ p_{AB} + p_{BA} = m_A v_A + m_B v_B \] \[ \frac{dp_{AB}}{dt} + \frac{dp_{BA}}{dt} = m_A \frac{dv_A}{dt} + m_B \frac{dv_B}{dt} \] \[ F_{AB} = -F_{BA} \]

Mass is the amount of matter in a person (\(100\,\mathrm{kg}\)) and does not change; weight depends on \(g\):

\[ w = mg = 100 \times 9.8, \qquad w = mg \leftrightarrow N \]

Friction

Friction acts along a surface.

  • Static friction resists the initiation of motion; it is proportional to the normal force (and so to weight), \(F_{\max} = \mu_s N\). If \(F_{\text{applied}} > F_{\max}\) the object will move (accelerate).
  • Kinetic friction acts on a moving object, with \(F_k < F_s\).

Section 6Work and energy

Work is action done on an object that displaces the object:

\[ w = F \times d, \qquad w = Fd\cos\theta \quad \text{(scalar)}, \qquad W = \int_A^B F \cdot ds \]

Only the component of force parallel to the displacement counts towards work (only \(F_x\) contributes). Work is \(+\)ve in the same direction as the distance and \(-\)ve in a different direction. Power is the rate of doing work, \(P = F \times v\).

Energy is the capacity to do work; \(w_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE\), the energy of motion. SI unit: joule (J) \(=\) N·m.

Kinetic energy — by virtue of motion

\[ w = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_i^{2} \qquad \text{(work--energy theorem)} \]

When an external force does work on a system:

  • \(w = +\)ve → work done on the system; the system increases its KE
  • \(w = -\)ve → work done by the system; the system decreases its KE

\(E_{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\); a more massive object has more KE.

Potential energy — due to position in a field

(Gravitational, electromagnetic.) As gravitational PE increases, KE converts down; away from the ground GPE increases (greater potential to fall), towards the centre of the earth there is less PE.

\[ E_{PE} = mgh \]

Forms of energy: KE, PE, thermal energy, chemical energy.

Section 7Simple harmonic motion

SHM is periodic and repeated.

  • Elastic potential energy → maximum when least or most compressed, where the direction changes
  • KE → maximum (block at the middle)
  • Sinusoidal: \(y = \sin x\)

Spring constant \(k\); the restoring force is always opposite to the motion:

\[ F_{\text{spring}} = -kx \qquad \text{(Hooke's law)} \] \[ \text{elastic PE} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^{2}, \qquad KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^{2} \ \text{at equilibrium } x = 0,\ PE = 0 \] \[ v_{\max} = A\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{T} \]

\(x\) is maximum and \(P\) is maximum at the other side of the oscillation. The total energy of a closed system is conserved (law of conservation of energy); total mechanical energy \(= \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}A^{2}\).

Free fall and the pendulum

PE → KE → ME, with \(ME = PE + KE\). For a pendulum: at the bottom KE is maximum and PE minimum; at the extremes PE is maximum and KE minimum. Resonance increases the amplitude of oscillation by applying a force at the resonant frequency.

\[ \tfrac{1}{2}mv_i^{2} + mgh_i = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^{2} + mgh_f \]

Non-mechanical energy: chemical, thermal, nuclear, electrical, acoustic.

Section 8Momentum, impulse and collisions

Momentum is a vector: \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) (kg·m/s).

\[ \text{Impulse} = F\Delta t \quad (\mathrm{N \cdot s}) \] \[ F\Delta t = \Delta p = mv_f - mv_i \qquad \text{(impulse--momentum theorem)} \]

Hence conservation of linear momentum: \((p_1 + p_2)_i = (p_1 + p_2)_f\), i.e. momentum before collision \(=\) momentum after collision.

Elastic collisions

The bodies remain separate after the collision; total KE and momentum are conserved.

Perfectly inelastic collisions

Two separate objects collide, after which they move together as one mass:

\[ p_1 + p_2 = p_{\text{total}}, \qquad m_1v_1 + m_2v_2 = (m_1 + m_2)v_f \]

KE is not conserved; momentum is conserved. In reality all collisions are inelastic; “elastic” and “perfectly inelastic” are approximations for mathematical simplification.

Section 9Circular and rotational motion

The instantaneous velocity (tangential speed) is constant in uniform circular motion, generated by the centripetal force. The velocity is tangent to the wheel: the magnitude is constant, the direction changing.

\[ F_c = ma_c, \qquad a_c = \frac{v_t^{2}}{r}, \qquad F_c = \frac{m v_t^{2}}{r} \]

Centripetal force is the force that pulls objects towards the axis of rotation during circular motion (e.g. tension, gravity, static friction). Centrifugal force is not a real force but a sensation: the inertia of your body attempting to maintain a straight-line path of motion — inertia follows a linear path.

Spin is movement around an internal axis; orbit is movement around an external axis.

Angular quantities

Angular displacement \(\theta\) is the angle swept out by a rotating body; 1 quadrant \(= \pi/2\), a full turn \(= 2\pi\) radians, \(+\)ve or \(-\)ve.

\[ \omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} \ \ (\mathrm{rad/s},\ \text{or rpm}), \qquad \alpha = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta t} \ \ (\mathrm{rad/s^2}) \]

Rotational kinematics

\[ \omega_f = \omega_i + \alpha t, \qquad \Delta\theta = \omega_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^{2}, \qquad \omega_f^{2} = \omega_i^{2} + 2\alpha\Delta\theta \]

Torque

How to initiate rotational motion? \(\tau\) (torque) is the ability of a force to rotate an object:

\[ \tau = F d \sin\theta \quad (\mathrm{N \cdot m}), \qquad \text{maximum at } 90^{\circ} \]

If it results in clockwise rotation it is \(+\)ve; if \(-\)ve it results in anticlockwise rotation. At equilibrium \(\tau_1 + \tau_2 = 0\).

Section 10Newton's law of universal gravitation

Satellites orbit around the earth: “they fall towards the earth but never hit it”.

Any object with mass will exert a gravitational force — you too exert a gravitational force, but it is negligible compared to the earth.

\[ F_g = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^{2}}, \qquad G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2} \]
  • Moon: \(F = ma\) → less
  • Apple: \(F = ma\) → negligible
  • Earth: \(F = ma\) → more considerable

In vacuum a feather and a bowling ball fall at the same time: more gravity is exerted on a more massive object, but more mass also means more inertia (resistance to being accelerated). With \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\),

\[ mg = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^{2}} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad g = G\frac{m_2}{r^{2}} \]

The mass of the falling object cancels from both sides, so it does not affect the rate of free fall.

Section 11Fluids

Fluids flow and alter their shape. \(\rho = m/V\); pressure is force per unit area, \(P = \dfrac{F}{A}\); and buoyancy is the upward force exerted by a fluid.

Archimedes' principle: a submerged object will experience a buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid.

  • \(\rho_{\text{ball}} < \rho_{\text{water}}\) and \(w_{\text{ball}} < w_{\text{water}}\) → the buoyant force will make it float
  • Steel is dense, but the air inside is not: \(F = mg_{\text{displaced fluid}} \rightarrow F = mg_{\text{ship}}\)
  • A floating object displaces fluid based on its mass
  • A sinking object displaces fluid based on its volume
  • Submerging: the volume of water increases precisely by the volume of the person submerged — Eureka!

For flow through a changing cross-section,

\[ P_1 A_1 V_1 = P_2 A_2 V_2 \] Back to top

Section 12Heat and temperature

Temperature is the amount of heat energy available for work in a system → the average KE of molecules in the system; more velocity means more KE. Molecular motion is translational, rotational and vibrational.

Hot → cool: through collisions, energy is transferred between systems until the temperature is equal — thermal equilibrium.

KE transfer is heat transfer. Heat is the amount of thermal energy which is added to or removed from a system; heat is energy in transit, and heat transfer alters the temperature of an object.

\[ KE_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac{3}{2}kT \] \[ \sum KE_{\text{molecules}} + \sum PE_{\text{molecules}} = U = \tfrac{3}{2}NkT \qquad \text{(thermal energy)} \]

Molecular potential energy is present within a molecule. Higher temperature → more internal energy.

A substance does not contain heat; it contains internal energy. Heat is the transfer of those (molecular KE \(+\) PE) from high temperature to low temperature.

The kinetic theory of gases ties pressure, volume and temperature together:

\[ PV = nRT \] Back to top

Section 13Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the study of heat and temperature and their relation to energy and work. Energy is conserved:

\[ \Delta PE + \Delta KE + \Delta U = 0 \]

\(U\) can do work → \(PV\) expansion, heat transfer. A state function depends only on the state of the system, not how it got there.

Zeroth law

If \(A \rightleftharpoons C\) and \(B \rightleftharpoons C\), then \(A\) and \(B\) are in thermal equilibrium. System and surroundings are separated by diathermal walls (allow heat flow) or adiabatic walls (do not allow heat flow).

First law — conservation of energy

\[ \Delta U = Q - W, \qquad dQ = dU + dW \]

Internal energy is energy transferred from or to the system as heat and as work (J): \(+\)ve into the system, \(-\)ve out of the system. Here \(dQ\) is heat, \(dU\) is internal energy and \(dW\) is the work done by the system.

Special processes

  1. Constant volume — no \(PV\) work done, \(W = 0\), \(\Delta U = Q\) (bomb calorimeter); isovolumetric.
  2. Constant temperature — \(0 = Q - W\), \(\Delta U = 0\), \(Q = W\) (isothermal; a car engine). Isobaric: \(W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV = P(V_2 - V_1)\).
  3. No heat transfer — \(Q = 0\), \(\Delta U = -W\): adiabatic process. Clouds change due to pressure difference.
  4. \(Q = 0\) and \(W = 0\) \(\Longleftrightarrow\) \(\Delta U = 0\) → isolated system.

Sign conventions: heat absorbed \(Q = +\)ve, heat released \(Q = -\)ve; work done by the system \(W = +\)ve, work done on the system \(W = -\)ve.

Second law

\[ Q_H = W + Q_L, \qquad W_{\text{net}} = Q_H - Q_C \]

100 % efficiency is not possible; there is no cyclic process for which every process is reversible (Carnot's principle) — friction and lost energy intervene. Heat cannot be transferred from cold to hot temperature (on its own).

Section 14Entropy

Entropy \(S\) (J/K) is a state function measuring disorder; it represents the inevitable partial loss of an engine's ability to convert heat energy to work.

\[ \Delta S = \left(\frac{Q}{T}\right)_{\text{reversible}} \qquad (\text{heat} / \text{Kelvin temperature}) \]

\(\Delta S = 0\) and \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} = 0\) for a Carnot engine. For any spontaneous process:

\[ \Delta S_{\text{univ}} > 0 \]

Disorder → \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} > 0\); order → \(\Delta S < 0\) locally, with dispersal of energy that increases \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}}\) as a whole. Things spontaneously form on a local level: water to ice, living organisms. Dispersal of matter and energy always involves an increase in entropy. An increase in entropy corresponds with a greater dispersal of matter and energy.

Microstates

A microstate is a specific configuration of all particles and energies in a system.

\[ S = k\ln W \]

where \(\ln\) is the natural log, \(W\) is the number of microstates, and \(k\) is the Boltzmann constant, \(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{m^2\,kg/s^2\,K}\).

Statistical mechanics tells us: “the distribution with the greatest number of microstates will be preferred statistically”. So finding the system in 2 and 2 (i.e. preferred) is 6 out of 16, 37.5 %. The probability is for the more dispersed microstate, therefore entropy tends to increase in the universe.

Third law

Temperature is a measure of available heat energy. As temperature decreases, internal energy decreases and KE decreases. Zero KE → \(0\,\mathrm{K} = -273.15\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), the solid phase. Entropy at \(0\,\mathrm{K}\) is zero: \(S = k\ln W\) with one microstate gives \(S = 0\) since \(\ln 1 = 0\). It is impossible to lower the temperature of any system to absolute zero in a finite number of steps (zero point energy).

Section 15Waves

Light and sound are periodic. Waves “originate by disturbance, propagate outwards”. A wave travelling through a medium is called a mechanical wave.

Transverse wavesLongitudinal waves (sound)
disturbance \(\perp\) to direction disturbance \(\parallel\) to direction, due to compression or rarefaction

The medium does not travel; only the energy is carried by the wave. Electromagnetic waves do not require a material medium — e.g. light, in a vacuum.

  • Amplitude \(A_0\) → distance travelled \(\perp\) to the propagation; the distance to a crest or trough. Greater \(A_0\) → more energy.
  • Period \(T\) → time required to complete one cycle.
  • Frequency \(\nu\) → number of waves per unit time; Hz \(=\) s\(^{-1}\).
  • \(\lambda\) → length of one cycle of the wave.
\[ \text{speed} = \lambda\nu \]

(Mechanical waves travel at a constant speed for a given medium.)

A travelling wave can be written as

\[ p(x,t) = A \sin(kx - \omega t + \phi) \]

Superposition and interference

Mechanical waves can overlap → superposition → interference pattern. Constructive interference adds (\(2n\)); destructive interference subtracts, and complete destructive interference (out of phase) gives less than the individual wave. Boundaries may be fixed, non-fixed, or both fixed (inverted). Diffraction → bending in corners.

Standing waves

Transverse, stationary waves between fixed ends: right- and left-travelling (reflected) waves interfere to give an interference pattern. The allowed modes are quantized in integer numbers of half wavelengths.

  • Nodes → zero amplitude (stationary)
  • Antinodes → points with maximum amplitude
  • More nodes, more energy
  • 2D (a drum) also has radial nodes; angular nodes → linear (1D); 3D has nodes that are 2D planes

Section 16Light

James Clerk Maxwell: light is oscillating electric and magnetic fields. Different frequencies give different colours.

  • Above visible frequency → ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma
  • Below visible frequency → infrared, microwave, radio

Radio → micro → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-rays → \(\gamma\) rays: \(\nu\) increases, \(\lambda\) decreases. Visible colours: ROYGBIV, low frequency to high.

Some materials can absorb only particular colours and reflect others — that is what we see. A green leaf: green is reflected, our eyes see it. If all \(\lambda\) are absorbed → appears black; if all \(\lambda\) are reflected → white, or some colour that is illuminating it.

Reflection and refraction

  • Reflection: measured from the normal, \(\angle i = \angle r\).
  • Refraction: bending of light as it travels from one medium into another (dense → less dense and vice versa).

For a thin lens or mirror, the object and image distances satisfy

\[ \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} \]

Colour mixing

R, G, B are the additive primaries; Y is the complementary colour of blue; the eye has 3 colour receptors. Subtractive primary colours → black; additive primary colours → white.

Section 17Electricity

Electric charge: like charges repel, opposite charges attract. \(e^- \rightarrow 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) is the fundamental charge (and \(p^+ \rightarrow +1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)).

\[ F = k\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^{2}}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99 \times 10^{9}, \qquad F_E > F_G \]

Point charge: \(E = k_e \dfrac{q}{r^{2}}\).

Electric potential energy

The field does work to produce acceleration:

\[ PE_{\text{elect}} = -qEd \quad (\mathrm{J}) \]

where \(E\) is the electric field strength, \(d\) the displacement from the reference point and \(q\) the charge on the object.

Potential difference:

\[ \Delta V = \frac{\Delta PE}{q} \quad (\mathrm{V = J/C}) \]

It is not a measure of energy but of its change in energy per unit charge. As charge flows it produces usable energy; current can be generated by either of these.

Current, resistance, power

\[ I = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} \quad \text{ampere (A)} = 1\,\mathrm{C/s} \] \[ R = \frac{\Delta V}{I} \quad \text{ohms } (\Omega = \mathrm{V/A}) \] \[ P = I\,\Delta V \quad \text{watts (W)} = \mathrm{J/s} \]

Current arises when there is net movement of electric charge. Resistance is the ability of a material to oppose charge flow. Electric power is the rate at which electrical energy is converted to other forms.

Section 18Magnetism

Circuitry → computing → engineering.

Magnet: magnetite (Fe3O4); Fe, Co, nickel. S–N — always both poles. Ferromagnetic: all the spins will align in the same direction.

The magnetic field of the Earth interacts with the high-energy charged particles which race towards us from the Sun and deflects them towards the magnetic pole → aurora borealis (the northern lights), by collision with atmospheric particles.

  • An electric current will deflect a compass needle.
  • Magnetic fields will affect the path of charged particles.

The electromagnetic force: a current \(I\) produces a magnetic field \(B\) — right-hand rule. Worked out roughly 1600 to 1900 by Nelson, Faraday, Huygens, Coulomb and Maxwell.

Section 19Beyond classical physics

Electromagnetism is described by Maxwell's equations, which explain electric and magnetic fields and the nature of light.

\[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0 \] \[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_v \] \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \] \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \]
