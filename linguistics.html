Linguistics

Linguistics is the study of language and its structure. It examines how languages are formed, how they change over time, how they are processed by the human brain, and how they are used in social contexts. What follows sets out the field itself, then the four ways in which language takes form — literature, sound, sketch and dnc — and finally what all four have in common.

Part I

The Field

How language is built, how it splits and drifts, and how it came to be written down.

The levels of language

Language is studied in layers, each smaller unit combining into the next. The layers are largely independent, which is why a sentence can be perfectly grammatical and still mean nothing, or be ungrammatical and entirely clear.

LevelStudies
Phoneticsthe physical sounds of speech — how they are produced and heard
Phonologywhich sound contrasts a particular language treats as meaningful
Morphologyhow words are built from smaller meaningful parts
Syntaxhow words combine into phrases and sentences
Semanticswhat words and sentences mean, in themselves
Pragmaticswhat a speaker means by them on a particular occasion
Discoursehow stretches of language hold together beyond the sentence

phoneme  →  morpheme  →  word  →  phrase  →  sentence  →  discourse

The unit that matters most is the phoneme: not a sound as such, but a sound contrast a language uses to distinguish words. The distinction is language-specific, which is why a contrast that is obvious to one speaker can be nearly inaudible to another.

A finite inventory — a few dozen phonemes, a few thousand morphemes — generates an unlimited number of sentences no one has said before. That productivity is the property linguistics is really trying to explain.

Branches of linguistics

The four questions in the opening paragraph are not loose description — each names a branch of the field.

QuestionBranchAsks
How languages are formed Descriptive and theoretical linguistics what the structure of a given language actually is, and what all languages share
How they change over time Historical and comparative linguistics how languages drift, split and relate to one another
How they are processed by the brain Psycholinguistics and neurolinguistics how language is acquired, produced, understood and lost
How they are used in social contexts Sociolinguistics how speech varies by group, setting, class and identity

Two more are practical rather than descriptive: applied linguistics, covering language teaching, translation and speech therapy, and computational linguistics, which builds systems that process language and in doing so tests how well our accounts of it hold up.

On the brain side, two regions have been known since the nineteenth century for the different ways their damage disrupts speech: Broca's area, where production breaks down while comprehension largely survives, and Wernicke's area, where speech stays fluent but stops carrying sense.

Families and change

Languages change constantly and unevenly, and because change accumulates, a single language spoken across a wide area eventually becomes several. Comparing the results lets us reconstruct the parent even when nothing of it was ever written down.

FamilyIncludes
Indo-EuropeanSanskrit, Hindi, Bengali, Persian, Greek, Latin and its descendants, the Germanic and Slavic languages
DravidianKannada, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam
Sino-TibetanChinese languages, Tibetan, Burmese
Afro-AsiaticArabic, Hebrew, Amharic, ancient Egyptian
AustronesianMalay, Indonesian, Tagalog, Malagasy, Hawaiian
Niger–CongoSwahili, Yoruba, Zulu and several hundred more

The reconstruction works because sound change is regular: it applies across the whole vocabulary rather than word by word, so correspondences between related languages are systematic and can be run backwards. Proto-Indo-European was recovered this way, from descendants spread from Iceland to Bengal, without a single surviving text.

Kinds of change
Sound changepronunciation shifts across the whole system
Semantic drifta word's meaning narrows, widens or moves entirely
Borrowingvocabulary taken from a language in contact
Grammaticalisationa full word wears down into a grammatical marker
Analogyirregular forms get regularised by pressure from the pattern

~7,000 languages spoken  •  about 40% with under 1,000 speakers

Every language that falls out of use takes with it a body of knowledge about place, plant, kin and story that was often never written down — which is why documentation has become one of the field's more urgent activities.

Writing systems

Speech is universal and roughly as old as our species; writing has been invented only a handful of times. Each system answers the same question differently: what size of unit does a single mark stand for?

TypeA symbol stands forExample
Logographica whole word or morphemeChinese characters
Syllabarya syllableJapanese kana
Abjada consonant; vowels mostly unwrittenArabic, Hebrew
Alphabeta single consonant or vowelGreek, Latin, Cyrillic
Abugidaa consonant carrying an inherent vowel, modified by marksKannada, Devanagari

The historical direction of travel is from picture toward sound. A drawing of a thing becomes a symbol for the thing, then for the word, then for the sound the word begins with — at which point the symbol has forgotten what it depicted and can spell anything.

pictogram  →  ideogram  →  logogram  →  syllable  →  phoneme

Which is where Part II begins: the sketch came first, and writing is what happened when sketching was made to carry speech.

Part II

Four Ways Language Takes Form

Literature, sound, sketch and dnc — four channels through which thought gets out of one head and into another.

Literature

Literature can be the basic form of human thought and culture. Speech and writing have preserved so much information about civilizations, present and older. It might have started as the expression of a single person, extended to a small community, and further formed a society based on its cultural significance.

Before writing

That sequence — one person, then a community, then a society built on it — is what oral tradition does, and it worked for far longer than writing has existed. Oral literature is held in memory by the shape of the language itself: metre, rhyme, alliteration and repeated formulaic phrases are all mnemonic devices before they are ornament. The Homeric epics and the Vedas were both transmitted for centuries this way, and in the Vedic case with an accuracy that written copying does not match.

Writing did not simply record what speech was already doing. It allowed a text to outlast everyone who knew it, be read out of order, and be argued with line by line — which is roughly the precondition for law, scripture and philosophy as we have them.

What written form makes possible

PropertyConsequence
Permanencea voice reaches people the speaker will never meet
Detachmentthe text can be examined apart from its author's presence
Revisionthought can be reworked rather than only re-performed
Accumulationeach generation starts from the previous one's record

Sound

Similarly, sound plays an important role in defining a culture. Remember whenever we hear an Egyptian theme, it always takes us back to that civilization. Even in the present age there are so many genres of music, but it all originates from sound waves of different pitch, frequency, wavelength and amplitude.

The four properties

PropertyPhysicallyHeard as
Frequencycycles per second, in hertzpitch — how high or low
Wavelengththe distance one cycle occupiesthe same information as frequency, inverted
Amplitudethe size of the pressure variationloudness
Waveformthe mix of overtones presenttimbre — why a flute and a violin differ at the same pitch

v = fλ  •  v ≈ 343 m/s in air  •  human hearing ≈ 20 Hz – 20 kHz

Pitch and wavelength are not two independent controls: fix the speed of sound and setting one sets the other. What genuinely varies independently is amplitude and waveform, which is why timbre — the overtone mix — is what actually identifies an instrument, a voice, or the "Egyptian theme" that places a whole civilization in two bars.

Sound carrying meaning

Speech uses these same properties structurally rather than decoratively. In tone languages such as Mandarin, pitch distinguishes words outright — the same syllable at a different contour is a different word. Elsewhere pitch and timing work above the word as prosody, marking questions, emphasis, irony and turn-taking. Prosody is doing a great deal of the work in ordinary conversation, which is why flat text loses so much and why the same sentence can be a compliment or an insult.

Sketch

How can we forget the cave paintings, which are the earliest descriptions of societies. Sketching shows us a wide range of information regarding a person's thought process or the ideology of a society. The canvas can be anything from two-dimensional sheets to three-dimensional wood, or it can be as simple as the sand on the seashore. It's the easiest way of the expression of our thought.

The earliest record

SiteRoughlyNotable for
Sulawesi and Borneo caves45,000+ yearsamong the oldest known figurative painting
Chauvet, France~32,000 yearsanimals rendered with shading and implied movement
Lascaux, France~17,000 yearslarge composed scenes across a cave system
Bhimbetka, India~10,000 years and latercontinuous use across many periods, including dance and hunt scenes

The handprint is the recurring motif across all of them, on separate continents, made by people with no contact — the simplest possible statement that someone was here. It is also the point where sketch and dnc meet, since the mark is a record of a body's position.

Why it is the easiest way

A sketch has an advantage the other three modes lack: much of it needs no shared code. A drawn horse resembles a horse, so it can be read by someone who shares no language with the person who drew it. Words cannot do this — they work only by prior agreement.

That advantage has a limit, and the limit is instructive. Resemblance handles objects well and abstractions badly: there is nothing that looks like "yesterday", "if", or "not". So every picture-based system that had to carry real language eventually borrowed sound, which is the transition traced in Part I.

Dnc

This one is interesting — it's called dnc, the dance of life. It's part of our life throughout our day, in the way we move and the things we do. It can be both static as well as dynamic. Static dnc is fashion and how we portray ourselves. Dynamic dnc can be the sports we play or the dance moves we make. It's the simplest way to express ourselves and our ideologies to the world around.

FormIsReads as
Static dncdress, grooming, posture, how we portray ourselvesa standing claim about who one is
Dynamic dncsport, dance, gesture, the way we move through a daysomething asserted in the moment

The body as a channel

Linguistics has names for both halves. Kinesics is the study of gesture, posture and expression; proxemics is the study of distance — how close people stand, which is regulated by rules nobody is taught and everybody notices when broken. Both vary sharply between cultures, which is exactly what makes them signalling systems rather than reflexes.

Static dnc has a linguistic parallel too. Sociolinguistics finds that speech carries the same information as clothing: accent, vocabulary and register announce region, group and affiliation before any content arrives. People shift between varieties depending on who they are with — code-switching — and getting dressed for an occasion is the same operation performed in a different medium.

Sign language is not gesture

One thing worth separating out carefully. Sign languages are full natural languages, not elaborate gesture and not dnc in the sense above. They have phonology built from handshape, location and movement, their own morphology and syntax, regional dialects, and they are acquired by children on the same developmental schedule as speech. They are also not derivative of the surrounding spoken language: British and American Sign Language are unrelated to one another despite both countries speaking English.

Which makes the deeper point about all four modes. Language is not tied to sound at all — it is a structure that can be carried by the voice, the hand or the page, and it survives the change of channel intact.

Part III

How Meaning Travels

What literature, sound, sketch and dnc share, and where they come apart.

Signs and semiotics

Semiotics is the study of signs in general, and it is the discipline that covers all four modes at once. Saussure split every sign into two parts.

PartIs
Signifierthe form — the sound, mark, garment or movement
Signifiedthe concept it calls up

His central observation was that the link between the two is arbitrary. There is nothing dog-like about the sound "dog"; the pairing holds because a community treats it as holding. This is why languages can differ at all, and why they drift — nothing anchors the pairing except agreement, and agreement moves.

Three ways a sign can work

Peirce then distinguished how the link is made, which sorts the four modes neatly.

KindWorks byExample
Iconresemblancea drawn horse, a portrait, onomatopoeia
Indexreal connection or tracesmoke for fire, a handprint on a cave wall
Symbolconvention alonethe word "horse", a national flag, a wedding ring

Sketch leans iconic, which is why it crosses language barriers. Literature is almost purely symbolic, which is why it does not. Sound and dnc sit across all three at once: a drum imitates a heartbeat, a limp indicates an injury, and a black armband means grief only by agreement.

Nothing means anything on its own. It means something to someone, under a code they share.

The four modes compared

Set side by side
ModeChannelLastsMainlyNeeds a shared code?
Literature voice, then page indefinitely, once written symbolic yes — entirely
Sound air, as pressure waves only as long as it sounds iconic and symbolic partly — some effects are direct
Sketch any surface that takes a mark as long as the surface does iconic largely not
Dnc the body itself the moment, unless recorded indexical and symbolic yes, and it varies by culture

Two things fall out of reading across the rows. The trade-off is between reach and independence: the modes that travel furthest in time are the ones that depend most completely on a code being shared, while the mode that needs least agreement — sketch — is also the one that struggles most with anything abstract.

And in practice the four almost never appear alone. A film, a ritual, a live performance or a page with a diagram on it runs several channels at once, each covering what the others cannot — which is what linguistics calls multimodality, and what the four sections above describe from the inside.
