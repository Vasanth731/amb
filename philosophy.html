Philosophy

Philosophy asks the questions that cannot be settled by measurement: what is right, what can be known, what makes a life good, whether anything we do is freely chosen, and what it means for a thing to exist at all. What follows works through it by field — ethics, the good life, the self and freedom, knowledge and language, then society and the thinkers who shaped each argument.

Different schools of thought in philosophy explore various ways of understanding existence, knowledge, morality, and society. Existentialism, associated with thinkers like Jean-Paul Sartre, focuses on human existence and the idea that individuals define their own meaning in life. Absurdism, often linked to Albert Camus, argues that the human search for meaning is ultimately futile in an indifferent universe. Nihilism, associated with Friedrich Nietzsche, rejects the existence of inherent meaning, truth, or value. Stoicism, practiced by philosophers such as Seneca and Marcus Aurelius, teaches inner peace through accepting what cannot be controlled and aligning oneself with nature. In ethics, Virtue Ethics, rooted in the work of Aristotle, argues that morality arises from a person’s character and virtues, while Consequentialism judges actions as right or wrong based on their outcomes, with Utilitarianism—developed by thinkers like Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill—seeking the greatest overall well-being. In contrast, Deontology, famously associated with Immanuel Kant, emphasizes adherence to duties and moral rules regardless of consequences. In epistemology, Rationalism, advocated by René Descartes, holds that reason is the primary source of knowledge, whereas Empiricism, represented by thinkers like John Locke, maintains that knowledge arises from sensory experience. Constructivism examines how humans actively construct knowledge and meaning from their experiences, while Skepticism questions accepted beliefs and suggests that absolute truth may be unknowable due to limits in perception and reasoning. Social and political philosophies include Critical Theory, developed by scholars of the Frankfurt School, which analyzes how power structures shape society; Marxism, based on the work of Karl Marx, which critiques the exploitation of workers by the ownership class and advocates collective control of resources; and Liberalism, associated with thinkers like John Locke, which emphasizes individual rights and liberties. Related views include Communitarianism, which prioritizes shared community values, and Individualism, which stresses the intrinsic worth and autonomy of the individual. In metaphysics, Idealism argues that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual, while Realism and Physicalism assert that the physical world and observable matter are primary, with consciousness emerging from physical processes; Naturalism similarly emphasizes understanding reality through scientific facts and experiments, while Spiritualism proposes that the spirits of the dead can communicate with the living. Finally, different approaches to reasoning and knowledge formation include Deductivism, which derives conclusions from logical rules; Inductivism, which infers general patterns from repeated observations; Abductivism, which proposes the most plausible explanation from limited evidence; and Probabilism, which evaluates beliefs and predictions in terms of likelihoods rather than absolute certainty.

Key philosophers across history have shaped major ideas about knowledge, morality, society, and reality. Socrates (c. 469–399 BCE) is considered one of the founders of Western philosophy, known for his method of questioning to examine beliefs and pursue truth. His student Plato emphasized wisdom, morality, and political philosophy through dialogues such as The Republic. Plato’s student Aristotle made foundational contributions to science, ethics, logic, and metaphysics, founded the Lyceum in Athens, and served as the teacher of Alexander the Great. In the East, Confucius (551–479 BCE) developed a philosophy centered on moral virtue, social harmony, and ethical conduct. In Christian philosophy, Augustine of Hippo helped shape early Christian doctrine by integrating classical philosophy with theology, while Thomas Aquinas, who studied under Albertus Magnus, systematized Catholic theology in his influential work Summa Theologica. In early modern philosophy, René Descartes wrote Meditations on First Philosophy and argued that reason is the foundation of knowledge. John Locke, trained in medicine, developed empiricism and political theories about natural rights, while David Hume, originally educated in law, advanced skepticism and empiricism about knowledge and causation. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, author of The Confessions, influenced ideas about freedom and society that later inspired the French Revolution. Immanuel Kant argued that although knowledge begins with experience, the mind actively structures that experience through innate concepts. Later German philosophy included Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, whose dialectical ideas influenced many later thinkers. Karl Marx, originally from a Jewish family that converted to Lutheranism, developed a critique of capitalism and theories about class struggle, while Friedrich Nietzsche sharply criticized religion and traditional morality. In the 20th century, Ludwig Wittgenstein made major contributions to analytic philosophy and the philosophy of language.

Part II

Ethics

Whether there are moral facts at all, and if so, what makes an act right and a distribution just.

Meta-ethics and moral facts

Meta-ethics is the deep layer: not which acts are wrong, but what sort of claim “that is wrong” even is. The dividing question is whether there are moral facts.

  • Moral realism → there are moral facts. Cruelty is wrong in the way that water is H₂O — independently of what anyone thinks.
  • Moral antirealism → there are no moral facts. Moral talk is doing something other than describing the world.
    • Moral subjectivism → a moral claim reports an attitude. “Cruelty is wrong” says something about the speaker, not about cruelty.

A second axis asks how far a moral rule reaches.

PositionClaim
Moral absolutism some acts are right or wrong in every situation, whatever the consequences or context
Moral relativism moral truth holds only relative to a framework; there is no view from nowhere
Cultural relativism the framework is the culture — right and wrong are what a society holds them to be
Cultural relativism has an awkward consequence: if a culture's own standards are the final word, then no culture can ever be criticised from outside — including for the way it treats the people inside it who disagree.

Divine command and Euthyphro

Divine command theory — the belief that what is moral and what is immoral is commanded by the divine.

Plato attacks this in the Euthyphro, a dialogue in which Socrates questions Euthyphro — his interlocutor — on the way to a courthouse. The trap Socrates sets is now called the Euthyphro problem, and it is a fork with no comfortable branch.

Normative ethics

Three families of answer to “what makes an act right?”, each locating rightness somewhere different.

TheoryRightness lies inAssociated with
Virtue ethics the character of the person acting Aristotle
Consequentialism the outcomes the act produces —
Utilitarianism outcomes, measured as the greatest overall well-being Bentham, Mill
Deontology duties and rules, whatever the outcome Kant

character → virtue ethics  •  outcome → consequentialism  •  duty → deontology

The three come apart in exactly the cases that matter. A lie that saves a life is forbidden by strict deontology, required by utilitarianism, and judged by virtue ethics according to what it reveals about the liar.

Justice and fairness

justice → still → fairness

The two words are close but not identical, and pulling them apart is the work of the subject.

AccountDistribute according toDifficulty
Justice as equality everyone gets the same identical treatment of unequal needs is not fair
Need-based justice whoever needs it most who verifies need, and does effort count for nothing?
Merit-based justice whoever earned or deserves it merit itself depends on unearned luck and starting position

Restorative justice

A different question altogether: not who gets what, but what happens after a wrong. If you do wrong, you try to make it right — you make amends.

If you make a mess, you should clean it up.

Notice how much this differs from punishment. Retribution asks what the wrongdoer deserves to suffer; restoration asks what the wronged person needs in order to be made whole.

Part III

The Good Life

What makes a life go well, who gets to judge it, and what to do if the universe supplies no answer.

The good life

A prior question hides inside this one: a good life judged by you, or by others?

The two standards can diverge completely. A life of quiet obscurity may satisfy the person living it and impress nobody; a celebrated career may look enviable from outside and feel hollow within. Whichever standard is chosen decides the answer before the details are examined.

The unexamined life is not worth living. — Socrates

Which supplies a third possibility. On Socrates' account the good life is not a set of contents at all — not pleasure, not achievement, not approval — but a relationship to your own life: having looked at it, questioned it, and understood why you live as you do.

Back to top

Eudaimonia

From Greek thought, and central to Aristotle: eudaimonia is a life of flourishing. Not a mood, not a run of good fortune, but a life in which a person is constantly striving at self-improvement — to be more virtuous, wiser, more thoughtful, more self-aware.

eudaimonia → flourishing → a whole life, not a moment of it

Two features follow. It is an activity rather than a state, so it cannot be handed to you or bought. And it is assessed across a complete life, which is why Aristotle thought you could not call anyone fortunate until their life was over.

Hedonism and the experience machine

  • Hedonism → good = pleasurable. Pleasure is the only thing valuable in itself.
  • Desire → what you are actually after → the thing the pleasure is attached to.

The experience machine

Suppose a machine could give you any experience you wanted, indistinguishable from the real thing, for the rest of your life. Brilliant career, love, achievement — all of it felt exactly as it would feel if true. Plug in and you never know the difference.

Most people refuse. Yet if pleasure were the only good, refusing would make no sense. The refusal suggests we want more than the experience of a life — we want the life to actually be happening, and our achievements to be really ours. It is a hard objection to hedonism, and it is usually credited to Robert Nozick.

Things are not always the way they seem — which is the argument's real sting. Inside the machine, everything seems perfect, and seeming perfect turns out not to be enough.

Stoicism

Practised by Seneca and Marcus Aurelius, Stoicism teaches inner peace through accepting what cannot be controlled and living in accordance with nature.

what is up to me  •  what is not up to me

The whole discipline rests on drawing that line honestly. Your judgements, intentions and responses are yours; reputation, health, weather and other people's choices are not. Distress, on this view, comes from placing hope in the second column.

Meaning, Sisyphus and the absurd

Sisyphus is condemned to roll a boulder up a hill; each time he nears the top it rolls back, and he begins again, for eternity.

Three responses

SchoolPositionAssociated with
Nihilism there is no inherent meaning, truth or value — and that is the end of it Nietzsche
Absurdism we need meaning, the universe is indifferent, and so the search for it is ultimately futile Camus
Existentialism no meaning is given, so each person makes their own Sartre

All three begin from the same observation — that things do not come with inherent meaning attached — and part company over what to do next. What matters, on the existentialist reading, is not the meaning the universe supplies but the meaning you are making of it.

Life is in your hands. You, and only you, have the power to make your life great — and only you can evaluate its greatness.

Camus, writing in the twentieth century, puts the responsibility in the same place: each of us determines the value of our own life. His method for living with the absurd is not resignation but persistence — keep asking why, accept a claim when there is evidence for it, and go on striving for truth even though no final answer arrives.

Notice that this returns the argument to the good life: if only you can evaluate your life's greatness, then “by you or by others” has been answered, and the burden of the judgement never leaves your hands.

Part IV

Self and Freedom

What makes you the same person over time, and whether any of your actions could have gone otherwise.

Fungibility and sameness

Fungibility — the property of being interchangeable with other objects of the same kind.
  • Money → carries value only → any ₹100 note is as good as any other → fungible.
  • A mug that was your first gift → an identical mug is not the same mug → not fungible.

The distinction sets up the harder problem that follows. For fungible things, “same kind” is all the sameness there is. For a person, it obviously is not — another human with your qualities would not be you — so something else must be doing the work.

Back to top

Personal identity

What makes the person reading this the same person who existed ten years ago?

Body theory

The same body, from birth to death. Simple, and immediately in trouble: the body's cells are replaced continuously, so the matter is not the same matter. Push on this and you arrive at the thought that what makes you you is not the physical stuff but the non-physical stuff — your consciousness.

Memory theory

identity → a chain of memory reaching back

You are the person whose experiences you remember. But if I lose my memory, am I the same person? And the chain has gaps everywhere — almost none of last Tuesday survives — without anyone concluding that a different person woke up on Wednesday.

Society is built on the assumption that individuals remain the same person over time, every time. Contracts, promises, punishment and gratitude all presuppose it, which is why a theoretical puzzle about identity is also a practical problem about responsibility.

Human and person

TermCriterion
Humanhaving human DNA — a biological fact
Personbeing part of our moral community — a moral status

Keeping these apart matters, because they can come apart. The two words are used interchangeably in ordinary speech, and nearly every hard argument in applied ethics turns on the gap between them.

Hard determinism

Hard determinism — the belief that all events are caused by past events, such that nothing other than what does occur could occur.

The principle of alternate possibilities

An action is free only if the agent — the person doing the thing — could have done otherwise.

free action → a genuine option existed  •  determinism → no option existed

Put those two together and the conclusion is immediate: if determinism is true, no action is free. That is the argument the rest of the debate is trying to escape.

Two kinds of causation

  • Event causation → no physical event can occur without having been caused by a previous event. Every cause is itself an effect, back without limit.
  • Agent causation → an agent can start a whole chain of causality that was not caused by anything else. The agent is an origin, not merely a link.

Agent causation is what defenders of free will need, and what makes critics uneasy: it asks us to accept that something in the physical world begins a causal chain from nothing prior.

Reductionism

The view that all parts of the world, and of our own experience, can be traced back — reduced down — to one singular kind of thing. It supports determinism directly: if you are entirely physical stuff obeying physical law, there is no room left for an uncaused decision.

Free will and compatibilism

How free you are depends on how many internal factors are influencing you, how many external ones, and how much of it you actually control.

belief + desire + temperament → action

Which is uncomfortable, because you did not choose your temperament, and your beliefs and desires arrived through a history you did not select either.

Libertarian free will

The belief that some human actions are freely chosen — genuinely open, not merely unpredictable. It requires agent causation, and it rejects the determinist premise outright.

Compatibilism

The move that accepts determinism and keeps responsibility anyway, by redefining what freedom requires:

You were responsible, because you did what you wanted to do — even though you could not have done otherwise.

Freedom, on this account, is acting from your own desires rather than under compulsion. A person who does what they want is free even if that wanting was itself caused. Critics answer that this renames the problem rather than solving it.

The story of Oedipus

Told that he is fated to kill his father and marry his mother, Oedipus flees to avoid it — and the flight is exactly what brings the prophecy about. Every choice he makes is his own, and every choice serves the outcome he was trying to escape.

That is why the story survives: it does not resolve the question. Oedipus acts freely at every step and cannot do otherwise in the end, which is precisely the position compatibilism asks us to find acceptable.

Where knowledge comes from, what reality is made of, how words carry more than they say, and whether beauty is in the object.

Kant's settlement is the one most later philosophy builds on: knowledge begins with experience, but the mind actively structures that experience through concepts it brings to it. Neither pure reason nor raw sensation on its own.

What is said, what is implied

Paul Grice named the gap between what is meant and what is said, and called the surplus conversational implicature.

TermMeaning
What is said the words that come out of the speaker's mouth — actual linguistic content
What is implied the actual words combined with the context in which we say them

Asked whether a candidate is any good, a referee who replies only that the applicant has excellent handwriting has said nothing negative and communicated a great deal.

Words that do things

With your words you can change the world. Saying “I do” at a wedding, or declaring war, does not describe a state of affairs — it creates one. Such utterances are called performatives, and they cannot be true or false, only successful or botched.

Words also build what was never there. Imaginary worlds — whole universes — can be created with our minds and then spoken about in detail, which is the problem the next two sections take up.

Grice's maxims

Four rules cooperative speakers are assumed to be following. They are not commands about politeness; they are the assumptions that let a listener recover meaning from words.

  1. Quantity
    • be adequately informative
    • do not be more informative than is required
  2. Quality
    • do not say what you think is false
    • refrain from claims for which you have insufficient evidence
  3. Relation
    • say relevant things
  4. Manner
    • avoid obscure turns of phrase
    • avoid ambiguity
    • be brief
    • be orderly
Violating these rules hinders communication.

Flouting a maxim

Breaking a maxim obviously, so that the listener notices, is not a failure of communication but a device. The referee praising the handwriting flouts relevance on purpose. Sarcasm flouts quality; a deliberately long-winded answer flouts manner. In each case the listener assumes cooperation is intact, asks why the rule was broken, and recovers the real message from the breach itself.

Reference and Meinong's ontology

“The unicorn in my garden is white.” There is no unicorn, so what is the sentence about? Meinong answered by splitting existence into three grades, each wider than the last.

Meinong's jungle

That is the nickname for the consequence: the outermost ring is overcrowded. The golden mountain, the round square, every object anyone has ever mistakenly described — a great many are in there, and most philosophers have found the population unacceptable.

Universe of discourse

The tidier repair. Fix the domain you are speaking about, and inside it assertions can be true or false.

ClaimDomain: the real worldDomain: the fiction
Harry Potter is a wizard false — there is no such person true — and everybody knows it

No new grades of being are required. What changed is the universe of discourse the sentence was evaluated against, which is why we can argue about fictional characters without confusion and without believing in them.

Aesthetic enjoyment and art

Aesthetic enjoyment runs through the emotions, which is what makes it hard to treat as a judgement about the object.

Source of beautyExample
Human-madea painting, a building, a song — art
Natural beautya coastline, a storm, a face — made by nobody

Two different questions

do you like it?   •   is it good?

These come apart constantly, and confusing them is the commonest mistake in talking about art. You can recognise a work as excellent and take no pleasure in it; you can love something you know to be slight. The first question is about you, the second claims something about the work.

Beauty is in the eye of the beholder.

Taken strictly, that saying answers only the first question and abolishes the second — if beauty is entirely in the beholder, no work is better than any other, and criticism has nothing to be about. Which is the same structure as moral subjectivism in Part II, transferred from goodness to beauty.
