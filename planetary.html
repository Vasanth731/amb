Planetary Sciences

From the first Planck second after the Big Bang to the last evaporating black hole — how space, time and matter came to exist together, how stars forged the raw material of planets, and where life might be hiding.

Space, Time and Matter

Space, time and matter all need to exist simultaneously, so that matter can be put in space at a time. Matter has mass, and matter warps space and time — that curvature is what we call gravity. Matter tells spacetime how to curve, and the warping of spacetime tells matter how to move. This is what explains why the planets revolve around the Sun.

In the beginning God created heaven and earth. Genesis 1:1 — beginning (time), heaven (space), earth (matter)

Forces of Classical Physics

The four forces unify as you look further back in time. All of them are different manifestations of the same force.

  • Electromagnetic + weak nuclear → electroweak force
  • Electroweak + strong nuclear → grand unified force
  • Grand unified + gravity → the single force that existed a few Planck seconds after the Big Bang

Standard Model of Particle Physics

Particles fall into two families: fermions, which make up matter, and bosons, which mediate the forces.

Fermions — make up matter

  • Quarks — up, top and charm (charge +2/3); down, bottom and strange (charge −1/3). Quarks bind into hadrons:
    • Baryons — formed by 3 quarks
    • Mesons — 1 quark + 1 antiquark
  • Leptons (not made of quarks) — electron, tau and muon (charge −1); electron neutrino, tau neutrino and muon neutrino (charge 0)

Bosons — mediate forces

  • Photons → electromagnetic force, mediated by γ
  • W−1, W+1, Z0 → weak nuclear force
  • Gluons → strong nuclear force
  • Higgs, and the hypothetical graviton

Symmetry, Quantum Fields and the Planck Scale

There is always symmetry in the universe: for every particle of matter there is antimatter of the same mass and opposite charge. Quantum field theory treats particles as excitations in fields, which paves the way for string theory.

Planck length  →  1.616229 × 10−35 m
Planck time    →  10−43 s
t = d / v,   c = 3 × 108 m/s

Many theories have tried to explain the beginning of the universe. The Big Bang is the one that still stands, and it is accepted by most scientists. So what came before it? At the Big Bang — the beginning, t = 0 — time itself began, so there is no question of a "before"; it doesn't make any sense. The thing to ponder is what happened one Planck time after the Big Bang, and how the universe has evolved over the 13.7 billion years since.

General Relativity and the Birth of a Star

Anything with mass warps spacetime, inducing a curvature that attracts all massive objects to each other. Roughly a billion years in, dense patches of gas were the seeds of the first stars.

A nebula sits in hydrostatic equilibrium: the outward energy (E1, gas pressure from the molecules) balances the inward energy (E2, gravitational force), so E1 = E2. Its atoms exist in the plasma state in dense, hot clouds. Once the cloud's mass exceeds the Jeans mass, gravity wins: inward pressure acts as a centripetal force while rotation supplies a centrifugal one. The cloud collapses, nuclear fusion ignites, energy pours out — and a star is born. Its radiation reionises the surroundings, making way for more stars to form.

Nuclear fusion works its way up through H, He, N, O and heavier elements. Iron is as far as a core can go; an iron core ends in a supernova, while a less dense core leads to a planetary nebula. The elements formed during fusion in stars become the raw material for planets and every other entity in the universe. Elements heavier than iron can only be forged in the burst of a supernova — and this is how planets are formed.

Stars exert gravity on one another and so form galaxies. A dwarf galaxy holds 100 million to 2 billion stars; a typical galaxy holds around 100 billion.

star → galaxy → groups → cluster → superclusters

Classifying Stars

The spectral sequence O B A F G K M runs from about 25,000 K down to 3,500 K.

  • O — blue, big, hot and bright; up to 200 solar masses
  • G — yellow, around 1 solar mass
  • K, M — red to dark red, small, cool and dim; down to 0.1 solar mass
  • Red giant — red and cool, 0.3 – 8 solar masses
  • White dwarf — tiny and hot, 0.2 – 1.3 solar masses

Lifecycle of a Star

The lifecycle of a star begins in a vast stellar nebula, a cloud of gas and dust spanning about 200 trillion kilometers. Under gravity, regions within the nebula collapse to form a protostar, roughly 100 million kilometers in size, which continues to contract and heat up until nuclear fusion begins, marking its transition into a main sequence star of about 1 million kilometers in diameter. As the star exhausts its hydrogen fuel, it expands into either a red giant (around 100 million kilometers) or, if it is much more massive, a red supergiant (about 1000 million kilometers).

A red giant of about 1.4 M☉ sheds its outer layers to create a planetary nebula, stretching up to 10,000 billion kilometers, leaving behind a dense white dwarf of about 10,000 kilometers, which eventually cools into a black dwarf of similar size. In contrast, a red supergiant ends its life in a dramatic supernova explosion, whose gases and clouds go on to form new nebulae. Its core collapses into either a dense neutron star, only about 15 kilometers wide, or, if massive enough, a black hole roughly 50 kilometers across with zero volume and infinite density.

Neutron star  →  core of 1.4 – 3 M☉
Black hole    →  core > 3 M☉, 0 volume, ρ = ∞
White dwarf   →  core < 1.4 M☉

How Life Began on Earth

The early Earth was devoid of life and full of volcanic activity, with an atmosphere of hostile conditions. The ingredients were there — H, CH4, CO2, N, NH3 and phosphate, plus water — and the Sun was the energy source. But the UV rays of that time were far too harsh, so the only protection was water: the deep oceans.

Hydrothermal vents — life in the dark

Sea water seeps into magma chambers and is ejected back at high temperature along with rich minerals, concentrating energy: Fe, Ba, Ca, Si, CO2, CH4 and H2. LUCA, the Last Universal Common Ancestor, would have lived in a hot, oxygen-free place like this, drawing energy from the chemical gradients across the vents. Genes shared between Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya are inherited from those ancestors.

  • Black smokers — acidic, CO2-rich water; Cu, Fe, S and CO2 contribute. Too hot for LUCA.
  • White smokers — the Mid-Atlantic ridge and the Lost City field. Alkaline, with lower CO2 — conditions that allow the formation of RNA.
And the earth was void and empty, and darkness was upon the face of the deep; and the spirit of God moved over the waters. Genesis 1:2

Dark Matter

Dark matter does not interact with light, is not baryonic matter, and does not emit photons. It is unaffected by radiation, which let it accumulate first and become dense, and it exerts gravitational force. Two lines of evidence point to it:

  • Gravitational lensing — light bending around mass we cannot see
  • Galaxy rotation curves — instead of following Kepler's laws, orbital velocity stays roughly constant with distance from the centre

It could be made of:

  • WIMPs — weakly interacting massive particles
  • Sterile neutrinos
  • Supersymmetric particles
  • Other exotic particles

Dark Energy and the Cosmological Constant

Gravity alone says that all galaxies should crumble together and end up as one — the Big Crunch, the opposite of the Big Bang. That does not happen. Instead a repulsive force dominates and the universe expands.

The cosmological constant Λ describes a constant energy density — vacuum energy, in energy per m3 — that fills up all of spacetime. It was introduced to support the steady state model, where attractive and repulsive forces both act to hold the universe steady. Λ must have been weak in the early universe, becoming more prominent as things grew more sparse, and now expansion is accelerating.

  • Dark energy — 68%
  • Dark matter — 27%
  • Visible matter — 5%

Hubble, Redshift and Recession

Edwin Hubble found that the emission spectra of galaxies are red-shifted — a Doppler shift. For a wave at constant speed, a greater wavelength means lower frequency and a lower pitch, while a shorter wavelength means higher frequency and a higher pitch. For light, a source moving towards us is blue-shifted and one moving away is red-shifted. Almost every galaxy is moving away, and recession velocity increases with distance.

v = H0 D   (v in km/s, D in Mpc)
H0 = 70 km/s per Mpc
1 Mpc = 3.26 million light years

Evidence for the Big Bang

Olbers' paradox

If space were infinite and filled with infinite stars, the night sky would blaze. Dark space tells us space is not transparent — there are neither infinite stars nor infinite space.

General relativity

Modern cosmology treats the universe as smooth: homogeneous (the same everywhere) and isotropic (the same in all directions). General relativity then makes it dynamic and changing — it must be either expanding or contracting. The cosmological constant Λ was originally added to cancel expansion against contraction and give a static universe.

Redshift, 1929

Hubble's red shift measurements showed the universe expanding. The rival steady state model answered with the C-field, in which new matter is created as the universe expands so that its density stays the same.

Penzias and Wilson, 1964

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were using a microwave antenna to study emission produced by Earth's atmosphere, looking for interference that would cause problems. What they found was uniform background noise in all directions — radiation left over from soon after the Big Bang. This is the cosmic microwave background radiation.

The Cosmic Microwave Background

The CMB sits at 2.7 K and is isotropic. It originates in thermal equilibrium about 377,000 years after the Big Bang, when electrons formed atoms and released photons as they descended to lower energy levels. Those EM waves were stretched out over billions of years, and now they arrive as microwaves.

  • Recession velocity gives an age of 13.8 billion years
  • H : He → 3 : 1 by mass
  • Baryon : photon → 3 : 1
  • The earliest galaxies are visible 13.4 billion light years away — 13.4 bya, and their light is only reaching us now

Particle accelerators add to the picture: colliding protons at high energy, with E = mc2, recreates conditions of the very early universe.

The End of the Universe

For the next few billion years, things stay much as they are. After that, two scenarios.

Scenario 1 — ultra-hot death

Expansion halts and reverses back to a single point: the Big Crunch. If the universe is elastic, it could bounce — but how many times has this already happened, and would everything and every law come out exactly the same?

Scenario 2 — ultra-cold death

Expansion continues indefinitely, with dark energy winning against gravitational force. All galaxies drift apart. All stars go away until only one exists and the others are an infinite distance off — and then that last star dies with no new star to replace it.

This is the end of the stelliferous era, about a trillion years after the Big Bang. Electrons and protons decay away, with no force left to compete with dark energy. Black holes evaporate through Hawking radiation. The universe reaches zero Kelvin, and spacetime itself is ripped off — the Big Rip.

A new universe with different characteristics could still emerge from quantum fluctuations.

The Shape of the Universe

The universe is open, flat or closed, and which one depends on its density — calculated from the mass and volume of galaxies. Curvature shows up in the angles of a triangle:

  • Positive curvature — angles sum to more than 180°  (Ω > 1, closed)
  • Negative curvature — angles sum to less than 180°  (Ω < 1, open)
  • Flat — angles sum to exactly 180°  (Ω0 = 1)
Ω0 = actual density / critical density

Supercomputer simulations of a flat universe reproduce the observed filaments, using roughly six times as much dark matter as baryonic matter. Combined with accelerating expansion and dark energy, this points away from a Big Crunch.

Extraterrestrial Life

Abiogenesis is the step from non-living chemistry to living biology. Astrobiology is the study of life in the universe — aliens included.

The habitable zone is the distance from a star where the surface temperature is 0 – 100 °C, so that water can exist as a liquid. But it isn't the only option: friction in Saturnian moons melts ice from within. Jupiter's Europa and Saturn's Enceladus are both candidates, and ammonia (NH3) can act like water too.

Does water really need to be in a liquid state? Could life instead take hold in interstellar dust that is rich in minerals? And of all the unicellular beings that might arise, how many ever get to be intelligent?

The Drake equation (1961)

N = R* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L
  • N — number of intelligent beings
  • R* — average rate of star formation
  • fp — fraction of stars that have planets
  • ne — fraction of those planets that are habitable
  • fl — fraction of those that develop life
  • fi — fraction that become intelligent
  • fc — fraction that could communicate with us
  • L — time transmitting, the lifetime of the civilisation

Are we all alone? For now, it is speculation.

Fermi Paradox and the Search

The Fermi paradox: the fact that we can't see or hear any other civilisations may indicate that we are the only one.

SETI — the search for extraterrestrial intelligence — has been listening for radio signals since the 1960s. Around 4,000 exoplanets have been found, detected by the dip in brightness when a planet passes in front of its star, and by measuring red shift and blue shift.

  • The closest system is 4 light years away: Alpha Centauri, with Proxima Centauri as its star
  • 20 July 1969 — the first Moon landing

Mars and Terraforming

Mars is next. It has no oxygen and it is cold, so the first step is pressurised, temperature-controlled chambers. Terraforming would go something like this:

  1. Ice and CO2 sit frozen in the poles of Mars
  2. Bombs could release it into the atmosphere
  3. That would unlock still more CO2 from the soil
  4. The thickening atmosphere heats the planet
  5. Water vapour condenses and forms oceans
  6. Plants begin to grow
  7. Humans can walk without a space suit

Surface gravity is g = 3.8 m/s2, and the whole process would take a long time — somewhere between 500 and 5,000 years.

Moons, Mining and Megastructures

  • Jovian moons — Europa, Callisto, Ganymede
  • Saturnian moons — Enceladus, Titan

Their low gravity makes them useful. Asteroid mining could support both living and energy production. A Dyson sphere — a ring built around the Sun, which could itself be lived on — is the extreme version: harnessing the energy of a star is crucial for interstellar travel.

Kardashev scale — a civilisation scale

  • Type 0 — humanity today, using crude forms of energy
  • Type 1 — planetary: uses all the star's energy that hits the planet
  • Type 2 — stellar: harnesses all the energy produced by its sun
  • Type 3 — galactic: harnesses the energy of an entire galaxy, black hole farming included
