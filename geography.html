What Geography Studies

Physical and human geography

Geography splits into two halves that share a single question: why is this here and not somewhere else? Physical geography covers landforms, water, climate, soils and ecosystems. Human geography covers population, settlement, economy, culture and politics. The interesting work usually happens where the two meet — a floodplain is a physical fact and a decision about where to build a city.

The five themes

  • Location — absolute, given by coordinates, or relative, given by what a place is near
  • Place — the physical and human character that makes somewhere distinct
  • Human–environment interaction — how people adapt to, depend on and modify their surroundings
  • Movement — the flow of people, goods, water and ideas
  • Region — areas grouped by a shared feature, whether physical, cultural or economic

Maps and Location

Latitude and longitude

Any point on Earth can be fixed by two angles. Latitude runs from the Equator north and south in parallel circles; longitude runs from pole to pole through the Prime Meridian at Greenwich. Latitude largely determines how much solar energy a place receives, which is why it predicts climate so well; longitude determines local time.

Equator    →  0° latitude, circumference ~40,075 km
Tropics    →  Cancer 23.5°N, Capricorn 23.5°S
Polar circles→  Arctic 66.5°N, Antarctic 66.5°S
Prime Meridian→  0° longitude; 15° of longitude = 1 hour of time

The Earth's 23.5° axial tilt is what produces seasons: it is not distance from the Sun but the angle at which sunlight strikes a hemisphere.

Scale and projection

Scale states how much ground a map distance represents — a large-scale map covers a small area in great detail, and a small-scale map does the reverse. Projection is the unavoidable compromise of drawing a sphere on a flat sheet: every projection distorts area, shape, distance or direction, and the only choice is which distortion to accept. Mercator preserves direction, which is why it was used for navigation, at the cost of hugely inflating land near the poles.

Modern practice has largely moved to GIS, which layers data over coordinates, with satellite remote sensing and GPS supplying the raw positions.

The Earth's Structure and Plate Tectonics

Layers of the Earth

crust → mantle → outer core → inner core

The crust is thin — a few kilometres under the oceans, tens of kilometres under the continents — and it is broken into rigid plates that ride on the slowly convecting mantle beneath. The liquid outer core generates the planet's magnetic field.

Plate boundaries

Almost every dramatic feature on the Earth's surface sits at a plate boundary, and the type of boundary determines what forms there.

BoundaryMotionProduces
Convergent Plates collide Fold mountains, ocean trenches, volcanic arcs, deep earthquakes — the Himalayas are India still pushing into Asia
Divergent Plates separate Mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys, new crust — the East African Rift and the Mid-Atlantic Ridge
Transform Plates slide past Shallow, often severe earthquakes with little volcanism — the San Andreas Fault

Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust through which lava, ash and gases erupt. Most sit above subduction zones or spreading ridges, which is why they trace out the Pacific "Ring of Fire" rather than scattering at random; a few, like Hawaii, sit over isolated mantle hotspots.

Forces That Shape the Land

Weathering, erosion, deposition

Tectonics builds land up; the surface processes wear it back down. The sequence is always the same, and every feature in the sections below is the product of one stage of it.

weathering → erosion → transport → deposition

Weathering breaks rock in place — physically by frost and heat, chemically by water and acids, biologically by roots and burrowing. Erosion then removes the loosened material, and deposition drops it somewhere else, where it may eventually become rock again.

The four agents

  • Rivers — cut valleys, gorges and canyons upstream; build floodplains, meanders and deltas downstream
  • Glaciers — carve U-shaped valleys, cirques and fjords, and dump moraines at their margins
  • Wind — the dominant agent in deserts, where it removes fine material and piles the rest into dunes
  • Waves — shape the entire coastline, cutting cliffs and caves in some places and building beaches and spits in others

Rock itself circulates: igneous rock forms from cooling magma, is broken down into sediment that compacts into sedimentary rock, and heat and pressure convert either into metamorphic rock — the rock cycle.

Two things are worth reading off this list. Mountains come in distinct kinds — fold mountains raised by collision, block mountains lifted between faults, and volcanic mountains built from their own erupted material. And plains matter historically out of proportion to how dramatic they look: flat, well-watered lowland is where agriculture and therefore dense population concentrates.

Water Bodies

The features

FeatureDescription
OceansVast bodies of salt water that cover most of the Earth's surface
SeasSmaller parts of oceans, often partially enclosed by land
RiversFlowing bodies of freshwater that move toward seas, lakes or oceans
LakesLarge inland bodies of standing water
PondsSmaller and shallower bodies of still water
StreamsNarrow, flowing water bodies, often smaller than rivers
WaterfallsPlaces where water flows over a steep drop or cliff
GlaciersLarge masses of ice that move slowly over land
Ice capsThick layers of ice covering extensive land areas, especially in polar regions

A river system has its own vocabulary: the source where it begins, the tributaries that join it, the drainage basin it collects from, the watershed dividing one basin from the next, and the mouth where it ends — often in a delta or an estuary.

The water cycle

evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff & infiltration → evaporation

Oceans hold ~97% of the Earth's water
Ice caps and glaciers hold ~2% — most of the fresh water
Accessible fresh water is well under 1% of the total

That last figure explains a great deal of human geography: settlements, borders and conflicts cluster around the small share of water that is both fresh and reachable.

Coastal and Ocean Features

FeatureDescription
BeachesSandy or pebbly shores found along the edges of water bodies
BaysCurved inlets of water partially enclosed by land
GulfsLarge and deep bays with narrow openings to the sea
StraitsNarrow passages of water connecting two larger water bodies
IsthmusA narrow strip of land connecting two larger land areas
PeninsulasLandforms surrounded by water on three sides
CapesPoints of land extending outward into a body of water
FjordsLong, deep inlets of the sea with steep sides, typically formed by glaciers
LagoonsShallow bodies of water separated from the sea by sandbars, coral reefs or barrier islands

Straits and isthmuses are the two features on this list that have shaped history most directly, because both are chokepoints: a strait is the only way through for shipping, and an isthmus is the only way across by land. Control of the Bosphorus, the Strait of Malacca and the Strait of Hormuz has mattered for centuries, and the Suez and Panama canals were built precisely to cut through isthmuses that stood in the way.

TundraCold, treeless region where the ground remains frozen for most of the year

Biomes and climate zones

These are not arbitrary categories — each is what grows where a particular combination of temperature and rainfall allows. Read together, they form a rough gradient from the Equator to the poles: tropical rainforest, savanna and grassland, desert, temperate forest, boreal forest, tundra, ice cap. Grasslands appear where there is enough rain for grass but too little for trees, and tundra is treeless because the subsoil stays permanently frozen as permafrost.

Wetlands are disproportionately valuable for their area: they buffer floods, filter water, store carbon and support dense concentrations of species — which is why draining them has proven costly.

Weather and Climate

Weather is the state of the atmosphere now; climate is the pattern of weather averaged over decades. Four factors do most of the work in explaining a place's climate: latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, and prevailing winds and ocean currents.

ZoneCharacter
TropicalHot year-round; either wet throughout or strongly seasonal rainfall
AridEvaporation exceeds rainfall; deserts and semi-deserts
TemperateDistinct warm and cool seasons; moderate rainfall
ContinentalLarge seasonal swings, far from any moderating ocean
PolarCold throughout; tundra and ice cap

The monsoon is the clearest case of geography producing climate. Land heats and cools faster than sea, so in summer the Asian landmass warms, air rises over it, and moist air is drawn in off the Indian Ocean; in winter the flow reverses. The result is a sharply seasonal rainfall that much of South and Southeast Asia has organised its agriculture around.

Continents and Political Geography

The continents

Based on the land features and the settlement of people, political countries emerged across Africa, Europe, the Middle East, Asia, Australia, Antarctica, and North and South America.

LandmassNote
AsiaLargest in both area and population; contains the highest and lowest points on land
AfricaStraddles the Equator, so its climate belts mirror north and south
EuropeNo physical separation from Asia; the division is historical and cultural
North AmericaJoined to South America by the Isthmus of Panama
South AmericaHolds the Amazon basin and the Andes, the longest mountain range
AustraliaSmallest continent; mostly arid interior with population on the coasts
AntarcticaIce cap continent with research stations but no permanent population or countries

The Middle East is a region rather than a continent — it spans western Asia and north-eastern Africa — and Antarctica, being governed by treaty rather than divided into states, is the one landmass where no political countries emerged at all.

Settlement and borders

Where people settled was never random. Early and dense settlement followed fertile soil, reliable fresh water, navigable rivers, defensible ground and access to the sea — which is why the great river valleys filled first and why most large cities today still sit on a coast or a river.

Borders drawn along rivers, watersheds and mountain ranges are called natural boundaries; those drawn along lines of latitude and longitude, typically by outside powers with a map rather than local knowledge, are geometric boundaries. Much of Africa's border network is of the second kind, a direct legacy of the partition agreed in Europe, and a good deal of subsequent conflict traces to boundaries that cut across the communities living on the ground.
