Algebra

Algebra serves as a gateway to mathematics. Patterns and problems can be written symbolically and solved using different algebraic concepts. Natural patterns such as flower petals are often described through sequences and series.

Sets and Number Systems

Every algebraic statement lives inside some number system, and each system is built by patching a gap in the one before it. The naturals \(\mathbb{N}\) cannot subtract freely, so we build the integers \(\mathbb{Z}\); the integers cannot divide freely, so we build the rationals \(\mathbb{Q}\); the rationals leave gaps such as \(\sqrt{2}\), so we build the reals \(\mathbb{R}\); and the reals cannot solve \(x^2 = -1\), so we build the complex numbers \(\mathbb{C}\).

\[ \mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C} \]

Counting the elements of overlapping collections uses the inclusion–exclusion principle:

\[ n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B) \] \[ n(A \cup B \cup C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) - n(A \cap B) - n(B \cap C) - n(A \cap C) + n(A \cap B \cap C) \]

De Morgan's laws relate unions and intersections through complements:

\[ (A \cup B)' = A' \cap B', \qquad (A \cap B)' = A' \cup B' \] Back to top

Sequences and Series

Arithmetic progression: \[ a_n = a + (n-1)d \] \[ S_n = \frac{n}{2}[2a + (n-1)d] \]

Geometric progression: \[ a_n = ar^{n-1} \] \[ S_n = a\left(\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\right) \]

Harmonic progression is the reciprocal of an arithmetic progression.

When \(|r| < 1\), a geometric series has a finite sum even though it never stops:

\[ S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}, \qquad |r| < 1 \]

Means and the AM–GM–HM inequality

Each progression has a matching mean, and for positive numbers they always fall in the same order.

MeanTwo numbers \(a, b\)General form
Arithmetic (AM)\(\dfrac{a+b}{2}\)\(\dfrac{1}{n}\sum x_i\)
Geometric (GM)\(\sqrt{ab}\)\(\left(\prod x_i\right)^{1/n}\)
Harmonic (HM)\(\dfrac{2ab}{a+b}\)\(\dfrac{n}{\sum 1/x_i}\)
\[ \mathrm{AM} \ge \mathrm{GM} \ge \mathrm{HM}, \qquad \mathrm{GM}^2 = \mathrm{AM} \times \mathrm{HM} \]

Equality holds only when all the numbers are equal — which is why AM–GM is such a common tool for proving that a quantity is minimised at a symmetric point.

Standard summations

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^3 = \left[\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right]^2 \] Back to top

Permutations and Combinations

Permutations and combinations: \[ nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!} \] \[ nP_r = r! \times nC_r \]

A permutation counts arrangements, where order matters; a combination counts selections, where it does not. The factor of \(r!\) between them is exactly the number of ways of ordering the \(r\) chosen objects.

\[ nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}, \qquad nC_r = nC_{n-r}, \qquad nC_r + nC_{r-1} = (n+1)C_r \]
SituationCount
Arrangements of \(n\) distinct objects\(n!\)
Arrangements with repeats \(p, q, r\) alike\(\dfrac{n!}{p!\,q!\,r!}\)
Circular arrangements of \(n\) objects\((n-1)!\)
Selections allowing repetition\((n+r-1)C_r\)
Total subsets of a set of size \(n\)\(2^n\)

Probability

Probability and statistics describe uncertainty in events.

\[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \] where \[ P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)} \]

From the conditional formula everything else follows. The addition rule handles "or", the multiplication rule handles "and", and Bayes' theorem reverses the direction of the conditioning:

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \] \[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B|A) = P(B)\,P(A|B) \] \[ P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)\,P(A)}{P(B)} \]

Two events are independent precisely when \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\), which is the same as saying \(P(A|B) = P(A)\) — knowing \(B\) tells you nothing about \(A\).

Binomial distribution

For \(n\) independent trials each succeeding with probability \(p\):

\[ P(X = r) = nC_r\, p^r q^{\,n-r}, \qquad q = 1-p \] \[ \mu = np, \qquad \sigma^2 = npq \] Back to top

Statistics

Standard deviation: \[ \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum x^2}{n} - \left(\frac{\sum x}{n}\right)^2} \] or \[ \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum d^2}{n}} \] where \[ d = x - \bar{x}, \quad \bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} \] and variance \(= \sigma^2\).

The mean, median and mode each describe the centre of a distribution but respond differently to outliers: the mean is dragged toward extreme values, while the median is not. For a moderately skewed distribution they are related empirically by

\[ \text{Mode} \approx 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean} \]

To compare spread across data sets with different units, the coefficient of variation expresses deviation as a percentage of the mean:

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sigma}{\bar{x}} \times 100\% \] Back to top

Logarithms and the Binomial Theorem

Logarithms and the Binomial Theorem introduce functions.

\[ (x+y)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} {n \choose r} x^{n-r}y^r \] \[ \log_a b = \frac{\log_c b}{\log_c a} \]

The general term of the expansion is the piece to reach for when a specific coefficient is wanted:

\[ T_{r+1} = {n \choose r} x^{n-r} y^r \]

Laws of logarithms

LawStatement
Product\(\log_a(mn) = \log_a m + \log_a n\)
Quotient\(\log_a\left(\dfrac{m}{n}\right) = \log_a m - \log_a n\)
Power\(\log_a(m^k) = k\log_a m\)
Change of base\(\log_a b = \dfrac{\log_c b}{\log_c a}\)
Reciprocal\(\log_a b = \dfrac{1}{\log_b a}\)
Identity\(a^{\log_a x} = x\)

A logarithm turns multiplication into addition, which is exactly why it collapses exponential growth onto a straight line — the reason log scales appear whenever data spans many orders of magnitude.

Polynomials

A polynomial of degree \(n\) has exactly \(n\) roots in \(\mathbb{C}\), counted with multiplicity. Two theorems make root-finding tractable: the remainder theorem says the remainder on dividing \(P(x)\) by \((x-a)\) is \(P(a)\), and the factor theorem says \((x-a)\) is a factor exactly when \(P(a) = 0\).

For a cubic \(ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = 0\) with roots \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\), the symmetric functions of the roots are read straight off the coefficients:

\[ \alpha + \beta + \gamma = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad \alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha = \frac{c}{a}, \qquad \alpha\beta\gamma = -\frac{d}{a} \] Back to top

Quadratic Equations

Quadratic equations:

\[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \]

Discriminant conditions:

\[ D = 0 \text{ (real and equal roots)} \] \[ D > 0 \text{ (real and distinct roots)} \] \[ D < 0 \text{ (complex roots)} \]

The roots also determine the coefficients, so a quadratic can be rebuilt from its solutions:

\[ \alpha + \beta = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad \alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a} \] \[ x^2 - (\alpha+\beta)x + \alpha\beta = 0 \]

Completing the square rewrites the same equation in vertex form, which shows immediately where the turning point sits:

\[ y = a(x-h)^2 + k, \qquad h = -\frac{b}{2a}, \quad k = -\frac{D}{4a} \] Back to top

Complex Numbers

Complex numbers:

\[ z = a + ib \] \[ \bar{z} = a - ib \] \[ |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \] \[ z = re^{i\theta} \] where \[ \theta = \arg z = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) \] \[ z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) \]

Writing \(z\) in polar form turns multiplication into rotation: moduli multiply while arguments add. De Moivre's theorem is the statement of this for powers, and it is what makes roots of unity easy to describe.

\[ (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta \] \[ z^{1/n} = r^{1/n}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right)\right], \quad k = 0,1,\dots,n-1 \]

The \(n\)-th roots of unity are these values with \(r = 1\) and \(\theta = 0\); they sit at the vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon on the unit circle and sum to zero. Some useful identities:

\[ z\bar{z} = |z|^2, \qquad |z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|, \qquad |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| \]

Setting \(\theta = \pi\) in the polar form gives Euler's identity, which ties together five of the most important constants in mathematics:

\[ e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0 \] Back to top

Trigonometry

Trigonometry describes the relationships in triangles and pyramidal structures.

\[ \sin\theta = \frac{O}{H}, \quad \cos\theta = \frac{A}{H}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{O}{A} \]

Fundamental identities

\[ \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1, \qquad 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta, \qquad 1 + \cot^2\theta = \mathrm{cosec}^2\theta \]

Compound and multiple angles

\[ \sin(A \pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A \sin B \] \[ \cos(A \pm B) = \cos A\cos B \mp \sin A \sin B \] \[ \tan(A \pm B) = \frac{\tan A \pm \tan B}{1 \mp \tan A\tan B} \] \[ \sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta, \qquad \cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta, \qquad \tan 2\theta = \frac{2\tan\theta}{1-\tan^2\theta} \]

Solving general triangles

Right-angled ratios only take you so far; for any triangle the sine and cosine rules apply.

\[ \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R \] \[ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \] \[ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}, \qquad s = \frac{a+b+c}{2} \] Back to top

Matrices and Determinants

Matrices and determinants:

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|}\,\mathrm{adj}\,A \] \[ \mathrm{adj}\,A = (\mathrm{cof}\,A)^T \] \[ AX = B \] \[ X = A^{-1}B \]

This inverse exists only when \(|A| \ne 0\), so the determinant is the quantity that decides whether a system of linear equations has a unique solution at all. Geometrically, \(|A|\) is the factor by which the matrix scales area or volume — a determinant of zero means the transformation flattens space, and information is lost.

\(|A|\)System \(AX = B\)
\(|A| \ne 0\)Consistent with a unique solution
\(|A| = 0\), \(\mathrm{adj}(A)B \ne 0\)Inconsistent — no solution
\(|A| = 0\), \(\mathrm{adj}(A)B = 0\)Consistent with infinitely many solutions

For small systems Cramer's rule gives the solution directly, where \(D_i\) is \(D\) with the \(i\)-th column replaced by the constants:

\[ x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{D_y}{D}, \qquad z = \frac{D_z}{D} \]

Useful properties

\[ |AB| = |A||B|, \qquad |A^T| = |A|, \qquad |A^{-1}| = \frac{1}{|A|}, \qquad |kA| = k^n|A| \] \[ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}, \qquad (AB)^T = B^T A^T \]

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

An eigenvector is a direction the matrix does not turn — it only stretches it by a factor \(\lambda\), the eigenvalue. These are found from the characteristic equation:

\[ AX = \lambda X \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |A - \lambda I| = 0 \] Back to top
