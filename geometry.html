The Coordinate Plane

Coordinate geometry, introduced by Descartes, turns geometric questions into algebraic ones by attaching numbers to points. Once that translation is made, a distance becomes a formula and an intersection becomes a pair of simultaneous equations.

QuantityFormula
Distance between two points\(\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}\)
Midpoint\(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\, \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\)
Section formula (ratio \(m:n\))\(\left(\dfrac{mx_2+nx_1}{m+n},\, \dfrac{my_2+ny_1}{m+n}\right)\)
Slope\(m = \dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} = \tan\theta\)
Area of a triangle\(\tfrac{1}{2}\left|x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2)\right|\)

Forms of a straight line

\[ y = mx + c \quad \text{(slope–intercept)}, \qquad y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \quad \text{(point–slope)} \] \[ \frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1 \quad \text{(intercept)}, \qquad ax + by + c = 0 \quad \text{(general)} \]

Relationships between lines

Two lines are parallel when their slopes are equal and perpendicular when the product of their slopes is \(-1\). The angle between them, and the distance from a point to a line, follow directly:

\[ \tan\theta = \left|\frac{m_1 - m_2}{1 + m_1m_2}\right| \] \[ d = \frac{|ax_1 + by_1 + c|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} \] \[ m_1 = m_2 \ \text{(parallel)}, \qquad m_1m_2 = -1 \ \text{(perpendicular)} \] Back to top

Circles

Circles can be expressed as \[ (x-a)^2 + (y-b)^2 = r^2 \] or \[ x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 \] where \((-g,-f)\) is the center and \(\sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c}\) is the radius.

A line meets a circle in two points, one point or none, and which case occurs is decided by comparing the perpendicular distance from the centre with the radius. The line \(y = mx + c\) is tangent to \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) exactly when

\[ c^2 = r^2(1 + m^2) \]

The tangent at a point \((x_1, y_1)\) on the circle is obtained by the rule of replacing \(x^2\) with \(xx_1\) and \(y^2\) with \(yy_1\):

\[ xx_1 + yy_1 = r^2 \]

The length of a tangent drawn from an external point \((x_1, y_1)\) is

\[ L = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2 + 2gx_1 + 2fy_1 + c} \] Back to top

Conic Sections

All the curves below arise from slicing a double cone at different angles — which is why one definition covers them all. A conic is the locus of points whose distance from a fixed point (the focus) is a constant multiple \(e\) (the eccentricity) of the distance from a fixed line (the directrix).

ConicEccentricityStandard form
Circle\(e = 0\)\(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\)
Ellipse\(0 < e < 1\)\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)
Parabola\(e = 1\)\(y^2 = 4ax\)
Hyperbola\(e > 1\)\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)

Parabola

A parabola is written as \[ y^2 = 4ax \] which represents a classic rightward opening parabola.

Its focus is at \((a, 0)\), its directrix is the line \(x = -a\), and its latus rectum — the chord through the focus perpendicular to the axis — has length \(4a\). The reflective property, that all rays parallel to the axis pass through the focus, is what makes this shape the basis of satellite dishes and headlight reflectors.

\[ y = mx + \frac{a}{m} \quad \text{(tangent condition for } y^2 = 4ax) \]

Ellipse and hyperbola

An ellipse is \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \] and a hyperbola is \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \] where \((ae,0)\) and \((-ae,0)\) are the foci and \((a,0)\) and \((-a,0)\) are the vertices.

The eccentricity of each is fixed by the relationship between \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ b^2 = a^2(1 - e^2) \quad \text{(ellipse)}, \qquad b^2 = a^2(e^2 - 1) \quad \text{(hyperbola)} \]

An ellipse is the locus of points whose distances to the two foci sum to a constant \(2a\), while a hyperbola is the locus where that difference is constant. The hyperbola also has asymptotes it approaches but never meets:

\[ y = \pm\frac{b}{a}x \]

Kepler found that planets travel on ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and Newton later derived that fact from his inverse-square law of gravitation — conic sections turned out to be the shapes of orbits.

Triangles and Polygons

Classical geometry concerns shapes rather than coordinates, and the triangle is its central object. Four notable centres arise from four different constructions:

CentreConstructed from
CentroidIntersection of the medians, at \(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2+x_3}{3},\, \dfrac{y_1+y_2+y_3}{3}\right)\)
IncentreIntersection of the angle bisectors; centre of the inscribed circle
CircumcentreIntersection of the perpendicular bisectors; centre of the circumscribed circle
OrthocentreIntersection of the altitudes

Pythagoras' theorem holds for right-angled triangles, and the cosine rule generalises it to any triangle — the extra term vanishes precisely when the angle is a right angle.

\[ a^2 + b^2 = c^2, \qquad c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C \]

For a regular polygon with \(n\) sides, the interior and exterior angles are

\[ \text{Interior} = \frac{(n-2)\times 180^\circ}{n}, \qquad \text{Exterior} = \frac{360^\circ}{n} \] Back to top

Three-Dimensional Geometry

Extending coordinates to space adds a third axis, and the same distance idea carries over:

\[ d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2} \]

A line through a point with direction ratios \(a, b, c\) is written in symmetric form, and a plane is written with a normal vector \((a, b, c)\):

\[ \frac{x-x_1}{a} = \frac{y-y_1}{b} = \frac{z-z_1}{c} \] \[ ax + by + cz + d = 0 \]

The distance from a point to a plane extends the two-dimensional formula by one term:

\[ D = \frac{|ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}} \] Back to top

Vectors

A vector carries both magnitude and direction, which makes it the natural language for geometry in space. The two products encode the two questions we most often ask about a pair of directions.

\[ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta \] \[ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta \]

The dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way and vanishes when they are perpendicular. The cross product produces a vector perpendicular to both, whose magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram they span. The scalar triple product gives the volume of the parallelepiped formed by three vectors, and is zero exactly when they lie in a common plane:

\[ V = |\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c})| \] Back to top
