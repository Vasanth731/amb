What Sociology Studies

Sociology studies how people and society work in the present, focusing on social institutions and patterns of behaviour. Where anthropology reaches back through evolution and material remains, sociology takes the society currently in front of it and asks why it holds the shape it does. Politics, sex, race, ethnicity and religion can all be understood through its concepts.

Macro and micro focus

The discipline covers both the macro and the micro focus of asking questions about society. A macro question asks how whole systems work: why a country's inequality is rising, how an institution reproduces itself. A micro question asks how interaction works up close: how two strangers negotiate a queue, how a classroom establishes who speaks. The same phenomenon usually needs both.

The sociological imagination

C. Wright Mills gave the discipline its central habit of mind: the capacity to see personal troubles as public issues. One person unemployed is a biography; ten million unemployed is a structure, and no amount of individual effort explains it. The move from the first reading to the second is what sociology trains.

Founders and Theoretical Paradigms

Comte and positivism

Auguste Comte, the founder of sociology, held that social phenomena can be studied through direct observation, and that these observations can be pulled together into theories. This is what makes data-driven research possible: gathering evidence in order to analyse it and test hypotheses, each a proposed relationship between two variables.

Structural functionalism

This paradigm treats society as a system of interlocking parts, each contributing to the stability of the whole. Robert Merton distinguished two kinds of contribution. Manifest functions are the intended consequences of a particular structure, and latent functions are the unintended ones. Schools are for learning — the manifest function — as well as for socializing, supervising children during working hours and sorting people into future positions, none of which appears in the curriculum. Social dysfunctions also exist: consequences that undermine the system rather than support it.

Émile Durkheim's work on solidarity, the division of labour and suicide rates established the approach's central claim — that social facts are real and cannot be reduced to individual psychology.

Conflict theory

Conflict theory states that we labour and change things around us in order to survive, and that while trying to free ourselves from these natural constraints we entangle ourselves in new social ones. Where functionalism sees parts cooperating, conflict theory sees groups competing over scarce resources, and asks who benefits from an arrangement that is presented as being in everyone's interest.

Karl Marx located the conflict in class and ownership of the means of production. The approach also deals with race conflict theory, which centres racial domination and resistance, and gender conflict theory, which centres patriarchy and the unequal distribution of power between men and women.

Symbolic interactionism

The micro-level paradigm: society exists in and through everyday interaction, and what holds it together is shared meaning. Max Weber insisted on understanding action from the actor's own point of view, and George Herbert Mead built an account of how the self arises out of taking the perspective of others. Erving Goffman later described social life as performance, with front stages, back stages and considerable effort spent managing impressions.

Research Methods

Research begins by turning a concept into something measurable — operationalization — then choosing a method suited to the question.

MethodGood forWeakness
SurveyPatterns across large populationsDepends on what people are willing and able to report
ExperimentEstablishing cause and effectArtificial settings; limited scope for social questions
Field researchMeaning and process in real settingsHard to generalise; observer effects
Secondary analysisHistorical and cross-national comparisonData were collected for someone else's purpose
Independent variable  →  the presumed cause
Dependent variable    →  the presumed effect
Reliability  →  the measure gives consistent results
Validity    →  the measure captures what it claims to

The standing warning is that correlation is not causation. Two variables can move together because one causes the other, because the causation runs the other way, or because a third variable drives both.

Culture

Symbols, values and norms

Symbols, values and norms are different in different scenarios, and the particular combination is what constitutes a culture. Symbols carry shared meaning, values state what is worth wanting, and norms specify expected behaviour. Norms come in degrees of seriousness:

  • Folkways — everyday customs; breaking them is odd rather than wrong
  • Mores — morally weighted rules; breaking them provokes real censure
  • Taboos — prohibitions so strong that violation is close to unthinkable
  • Laws — norms formally codified and enforced by the state

Culture, subculture, counterculture

Difference in symbols, values and norms leads to culture, subculture — a group with distinct patterns within a wider society — and counterculture, whose values stand deliberately opposed to the mainstream. Culture divides into the material, the physical things a society produces, and the non-material: its ideas, beliefs and practices. The two change at different speeds, producing what William Ogburn called cultural lag — technology arriving before the norms for using it.

Socialization

Nature and nurture

Nurture is behaviour based on the people and environment around us. Sociology does not deny biology, but its contribution is to show how much of what feels innate — taste, ambition, accent, posture, what counts as common sense — is learned from a particular position in a particular society. Cases of extreme isolation demonstrate how little develops without it.

Developing a self

Social development, socialization and interaction lead us from small structures to larger ones.

interaction → group → institution → society

Charles Horton Cooley described the looking-glass self: we come to see ourselves as we believe others see us. Mead distinguished the spontaneous I from the socially formed me, and argued that children develop a self by learning to take the role of the other, first in imitation, then in play, then in organised games with rules. The agents of socialization are family first, then school, peer group, workplace and media.

Anticipatory and class socialization

Anticipatory socialization is learning to fit into a group you will someday be a part of — rehearsing the manner of a profession before entering it. Class socialization is the most common form, passed from parents to children: not just resources but dispositions, speech, expectations and a sense of what is realistic to want. Resocialization is the deliberate replacement of one set of norms with another, which is what Goffman's total institutions — barracks, monasteries, prisons — are built to accomplish.

Groups and Organizations

Social groups

A social group is a collection of people who have something in common that is significant to them. Primary groups are small, lasting and emotionally central; secondary groups are larger and goal-oriented. In-groups command loyalty while out-groups attract suspicion, and a reference group is whichever group a person measures themselves against — often one they do not belong to.

Size changes everything: a dyad of two is intense but fragile, since either member's exit ends it, while a triad of three gains stability and the possibility of two-against-one.

Formal, normative and coercive

Formal organizations are formed to achieve goals efficiently. Within them:

  • Normative organizations are ones people join as volunteers to pursue a goal they consider morally worthwhile
  • Coercive organizations are those like jails, where people have no say in whether they join
  • Utilitarian organizations are joined for material reward — most workplaces

Oligarchy and bureaucracy

An oligarchy is where the people at the top make decisions for everyone at the bottom, a hierarchy which gives rise to bureaucracies. Weber described the bureaucratic form by its specialisation, its hierarchy of offices, its written rules, its impersonality and its hiring on technical qualification rather than personal connection.

Its virtue is that it treats like cases alike; its costs are ritualism, inefficiency and alienation, and its tendency, as Robert Michels argued, is for power to concentrate at the top regardless of how democratic the organisation's founding intentions were.

Deviance and Social Control

Deviants are non-normative — they depart from the expectations of their society. Deviance is therefore relative: nothing is deviant in itself, only in relation to a set of norms, which is why the same act can be criminal in one place and unremarkable in another.

Sanctions are responses to conformity and its absence. Positive sanctions reward compliance and negative sanctions punish departure; each can be formal, delivered by an institution, or informal, delivered by a raised eyebrow.

Social Stratification

Social stratification is a system by which a society categorizes people and ranks them in a hierarchy. It is a property of societies rather than individuals, it persists across generations, it is supported by beliefs that present it as natural or deserved, and it applies to whole categories of people rather than to particular ones.

Caste, class and apartheid

Apartheid and the caste system are among the clearest examples. Caste systems assign position at birth and hold it for life, tying occupation, marriage and contact to inherited rank. Apartheid in South Africa was stratification written directly into law by race. Class systems permit movement in principle and justify themselves through meritocracy — the claim that position reflects talent and effort.

Mobility and its limits

Social mobility is movement between positions: upward or downward, intragenerational within one life, or intergenerational between parents and children. The Davis–Moore thesis argues stratification is functional because it rewards the work most important to a society; critics reply that reward tracks power and inheritance far more closely than it tracks importance, and point to the gap between income and accumulated wealth as the mechanism by which advantage persists.

Gender, Sex, Race and Ethnicity

Gender and sex

Gender is a social convention while sex refers to biological categorisation. What follows from the distinction is that expectations attached to gender — occupations, temperament, responsibility for care — are social arrangements rather than biological necessities, and they vary between societies and shift over time. Patriarchy names a system in which men hold disproportionate power, and sociologists study it through measurable traces: pay gaps, the division of unpaid domestic labour, and representation in positions of authority.

Race and ethnicity

Ethnicities are socially constructed categories based on cultural traits and a shared cultural heritage, rather than on visual or biological traits. Race is based on observable, physical traits. Both are socially constructed, but that does not make them unreal in their effects: racial categories vary between countries and change over time, yet they reliably predict differences in income, health, education and treatment by the state.

A minority in the sociological sense is defined by subordinate position rather than by numbers. Prejudice is the attitude, discrimination the action, and institutional discrimination the version built into ordinary procedures, which continues to produce unequal outcomes without requiring anyone to hold prejudiced views.

Family, Religion, Education and Politics

Kinship and family

Kinship is a social bond based on common ancestry, marriage or adoption — which already shows that it is not purely biological. Fictive kin is what we have when friends become family, and it does much of the same work: obligation, support, a claim on someone's time. Family forms vary widely, from nuclear to extended, and the marriage rules attached to them — who may marry whom, and where a couple lives afterwards — differ from society to society.

Religion

Durkheim treated religion as the community's way of representing itself to itself, dividing the world into the sacred and the profane and binding people through shared ritual. Weber traced how a religious ethic could shape economic behaviour, in his account of the Protestant ethic and the development of capitalism. Contemporary work centres on secularization, on religion's continuing role in identity and politics, and on the difference between belief, belonging and practice, which do not decline together.

Education

Education is a social institution through which society provides its members with all kinds of important knowledge. Its latent functions are as consequential as its manifest one: the hidden curriculum teaches punctuality, deference and how to wait; tracking sorts pupils into paths that tend to confirm where they started; and credentialism makes qualifications a gate to employment independent of the skills they certify.

Politics and authority

Politics is the social organisation of power, the ability to achieve an aim despite resistance. Weber distinguished power from authority, which is power accepted as legitimate, and identified three grounds on which that acceptance rests:

Traditional   →  legitimacy from custom and inheritance
Rational–legal →  legitimacy from rules and offices
Charismatic  →  legitimacy from the personal qualities of a leader

States are then classified by how power is held and constrained — democratic, authoritarian, totalitarian — and sociology studies not only formal institutions but the distribution of power beneath them: who is organised, who participates, and whose interests are represented when decisions are made.
