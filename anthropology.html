Anthropology

The study of human culture from a historical and evolutionary perspective — how we became the kind of animal that has culture at all, how we read the lives of people who left nothing behind but ruins and objects, and how societies develop and adapt over time.

What Anthropology Studies

Anthropology asks what it means to be human, and answers by comparison. Where most disciplines take one slice of human life, anthropology insists on the whole of it: biology and belief, bones and grammar, the last four million years and the village down the road. By examining social practices, belief systems, languages, human evolution and material remains, it builds a comprehensive picture of how societies develop and adapt over time, and it helps us understand the cultures around the world rather than only our own.

Holism and the comparative method

Two commitments define the field. Holism is the refusal to separate the biological from the cultural — a human diet is at once a nutritional fact, an economic system and a set of taboos. The comparative method is the habit of testing any claim about "human nature" against the widest possible range of societies, because a pattern found in one place tells you nothing until you know whether it holds elsewhere.

Relativism and ethnocentrism

Ethnocentrism is judging another culture by the standards of your own. Cultural relativism is the discipline's corrective: understanding a practice first in its own terms and its own context before evaluating it. Fieldworkers distinguish the emic view, the account insiders give of their own lives, from the etic view, the analytical account an outside observer constructs.

Relativism as a method is not the same as relativism as a morality, and anthropologists argue about where the line falls — particularly over practices that cause harm.

The Four Branches

The four branches are cultural, archaeology, biological and linguistic. Each takes a different kind of evidence, and the field's claim is that the four together say more than any one alone.

Cultural anthropology

Also called social anthropology, this branch studies living societies — their kinship, politics, economics, religion and everyday practice — primarily through long-term fieldwork. Its characteristic product is the ethnography, a detailed written account of one community, and its characteristic move is to use that one case to unsettle assumptions that seemed universal.

Archaeology

We understand people who lived years ago through the ruins and objects they left behind. Archaeology recovers and interprets that material record: tools, pottery, hearths, burials, buildings, seeds, bones and rubbish. Rubbish is often the most informative of these, because nobody curates a midden for posterity.

Biological anthropology

Sometimes called physical anthropology, this branch covers human evolution, primatology, human variation, genetics, growth and adaptation, and forensic identification. It supplies the deep timeline the other branches work within.

Linguistic anthropology

Language is treated here not as an abstract system but as social action: how people actually speak, what speaking does, how languages are related, and how they change and die.

A fifth, applied anthropology, is often added — the use of all four in practical settings such as public health, development, forensics, heritage management and product design.

Human Evolution

The hominin line

Humans did not descend from chimpanzees; the two share a common ancestor roughly 6 to 7 million years ago. What followed was not a ladder but a bush, with several hominin species often alive at the same time.

Species When Notable for
Sahelanthropus ~7 mya Among the earliest candidates after the split from other apes
Australopithecus 4 – 2 mya Fully bipedal with a small brain; includes "Lucy" and the Laetoli footprints
Homo habilis 2.4 – 1.4 mya Associated with the first systematic stone tools
Homo erectus 1.9 mya – 100 kya First to leave Africa; controlled use of fire; body proportions like ours
Homo heidelbergensis 700 – 200 kya Large-brained; likely ancestral to both Neanderthals and us
Homo neanderthalensis 400 – 40 kya Cold-adapted Eurasians; burials, pigments, complex tools
Homo sapiens ~300 kya – now Originating in Africa; the only surviving hominin

Bipedalism, brains and tools

Three changes matter most, and they did not happen together. Walking upright came first, by several million years, freeing the hands and reshaping the pelvis — which narrowed the birth canal and pushed human infants toward being born helpless. Encephalization, the growth in brain size relative to body, came later and was expensive: the brain is about 2% of body weight and burns roughly 20% of resting energy. Cooking, which pre-digests food outside the body, is one leading explanation for how that bill was paid.

bipedalism → free hands → tools → cooking → bigger brains → longer childhood

Out of Africa

Homo erectus spread across Eurasia first. Our own species left Africa in a major dispersal roughly 60,000 to 70,000 years ago, reaching Australia by around 50,000 years ago and the Americas by at least 15,000 years ago. Ancient DNA has since shown that the newcomers interbred with the populations they met: most people outside Africa carry a small percentage of Neanderthal ancestry, and some populations carry Denisovan ancestry too.

The Three Revolutions

In Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind, Yuval Noah Harari argues that the cognitive, agricultural and scientific revolutions are the main few turning points in human history — each one changing not just what people could do but what they could imagine doing.

Cognitive revolution

Around 70,000 years ago, Homo sapiens began behaving in ways no other species had: art, ornament, long-distance trade, burial with grave goods, boats. Harari's proposal is that the key new capacity was fiction — the ability to speak about things that do not physically exist. Gods, nations, laws and money are all of this kind, and they allow strangers to cooperate in very large numbers on the strength of a shared story.

Agricultural revolution

From about 12,000 years ago, societies in several independent centres — the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, New Guinea — shifted from foraging to farming. Domesticated plants and animals produced a surplus, and surplus made possible permanent settlement, storage, specialists, taxation, writing and states.

Harari calls this "history's biggest fraud", and the skeletal evidence gives the claim some support: early farmers were typically shorter, worked longer hours, ate a narrower diet and carried more infectious disease and tooth decay than the foragers before them. Wheat did very well; individual humans did worse, even as their numbers grew.

Scientific revolution

From roughly 500 years ago, a new willingness to admit ignorance, to use mathematics, and to convert knowledge into power set off a feedback loop between science, empire and capital that has not yet stopped. Anthropology itself is a product of this period — which is part of why the discipline is uneasy about its own colonial origins.

Reading the Material Record

Stratigraphy and context

The single most important principle is context: an object's meaning lies in where it was found, what lay beside it and what lay above and below it. A beautiful pot with no recorded position is nearly mute, which is why looting destroys knowledge even when the object survives.

Stratigraphy applies the geological law of superposition — lower layers are older — to human deposits. Seriation orders sites by how the styles of their artefacts change, and typology groups artefacts into classes so they can be compared across sites at all.

Dating methods

Radiocarbon (14C)  →  half-life 5,730 years; organic material up to ~50,000 years
Dendrochronology  →  tree rings; year-by-year precision where wood survives
Potassium–argon  →  volcanic rock; millions of years, used for early hominins
Thermoluminescence  →  fired pottery and burnt flint

Alongside dating, other techniques reconstruct life rather than time: pollen and seed analysis for environment and diet, isotope analysis of teeth for where a person grew up, ethnoarchaeology — watching living craftspeople to understand the traces their work leaves — and, increasingly, ancient DNA.

Culture, Kinship and Belief

Symbols, norms and values

Culture is learned, shared and symbolic. It is transmitted between generations rather than inherited biologically, which is why it can change far faster than genes can. It divides into the material — the objects a society makes — and the non-material: its symbols, values, norms, beliefs and language.

Kinship and descent

Every society organises relatedness, but none does it the same way, and the categories are social rather than simply biological. Descent may be traced through the father (patrilineal), the mother (matrilineal), both (bilateral) or by choice. Residence after marriage may be with the husband's kin, the wife's kin, or neither.

The terms themselves reveal the system: English collapses many relatives into "cousin", while other languages distinguish mother's brother's child from father's brother's child, because the two stand in entirely different social relations to you.

Belief and ritual

Belief systems range from animism, in which spirit inhabits the world at large, through totemism, which links social groups to natural species, to the organised world religions. What anthropology attends to is less doctrine than practice: what ritual does for the people performing it.

Arnold van Gennep showed that rites of passage the world over share three phases, and Victor Turner developed the middle one into a general account of how societies manage change.

separation → liminality → reincorporation

Exchange and economy

Not all economies are markets. Anthropologists distinguish reciprocity (direct give-and-take between parties), redistribution (goods collected centrally, then handed back out) and market exchange (price-setting through supply and demand). Marcel Mauss's work on the gift showed that a present is rarely free: it creates an obligation, and the obligation creates the relationship.

Language and Thought

Language is the medium culture travels in, so its history is a record of human history. Historical linguistics reconstructs relationships between languages by comparing systematic sound correspondences, and can recover vocabulary from languages never written down — Proto-Indo-European being the classic case, inferred from descendants spanning Iceland to Bengal.

The Sapir–Whorf hypothesis asks whether the language you speak shapes how you think. Its strong form, that language determines thought, is not supported; a weaker version, in which habitual linguistic categories nudge attention and memory, has held up in studies of colour, space and time.

~7,000 languages spoken today
roughly 40% have fewer than 1,000 speakers
a language is estimated to fall out of use every few weeks

Every language that dies takes with it a body of knowledge about place, plant, kin and story that was never written down — which is why documentation work sits at the centre of the linguistic branch.

Fieldwork and the Discipline

Participant observation

The signature method is to live for an extended period among the people being studied, learning the language and joining in daily life while recording it. The method's strength is access to what people actually do rather than what they say they do; its difficulty is that the observer is part of what is observed. Contemporary practice therefore treats reflexivity — accounting for the fieldworker's own position and effect — as part of the evidence rather than a flaw in it.

Ethics has moved to the centre of the field: informed consent, community benefit, the repatriation of human remains and artefacts, and honesty about the discipline's history of working alongside colonial administrations.
