Origins and Schools of Thought

Structuralism, functionalism, psychoanalysis

The discipline begins with three answers to the same question. Structuralism, associated with Wilhelm Wundt and Edward Titchener, tried to break consciousness into its elementary sensations by asking trained observers to report on their own inner experience. Functionalism, associated with William James, objected that this misses the point: a mind is for something, and the question is what mental processes accomplish. Psychoanalysis, Sigmund Freud's contribution, held that behaviour is largely driven by unconscious conflict inaccessible to introspection at all.

Behaviourism to the biopsychosocial model

Behaviourism then rejected inner experience as unmeasurable and studied only observable responses; the cognitive revolution of the 1950s and 60s brought the mind back as an information-processing system; humanistic psychology insisted on growth and meaning; and evolutionary and biological approaches asked what the mind was built for. Contemporary practice mostly declines to choose, using the biopsychosocial model, which treats every phenomenon as having biological, psychological and social contributions at once.

The Biological Basis

Neurons and neurotransmitters

All brain activity and feeling comes down to the brain and how it is connected through the nervous system: neurons signalling to one another with neurotransmitters and hormones such as the endorphins. A neuron fires all-or-nothing — an action potential travels down the axon, and chemical messengers cross the synapse to the next cell, where they either excite or inhibit it.

Messenger Associated with
EndorphinsPain relief and pleasure; the body's own opiates
DopamineReward, motivation, movement; implicated in addiction and schizophrenia
SerotoninMood, appetite, sleep; a target of many antidepressants
AcetylcholineMuscle action, learning and memory
GABAThe main inhibitory messenger; too little is linked to anxiety and seizures
NorepinephrineAlertness and arousal
CortisolA hormone, not a transmitter — the body's main stress signal

The nervous system

central (brain + spinal cord)  |  peripheral → somatic (voluntary) · autonomic → sympathetic (arousing) · parasympathetic (calming)

The sympathetic branch produces the fight-or-flight response; the parasympathetic returns the body to baseline afterwards. Much of what is experienced as an emotion is this system's activity being noticed.

Regions of the brain

  • Brainstem — breathing, heartbeat, arousal; also gates signals during sleep
  • Cerebellum — balance, coordination, procedural learning
  • Limbic system — amygdala (fear, threat), hippocampus (forming new memories), hypothalamus (drives, hormones)
  • Motor cortex — issues commands for voluntary movement
  • Frontal lobe — planning, judgement, inhibition; the last region to finish developing
  • Occipital, temporal and parietal lobes — vision, hearing and language, touch and spatial sense

Neuroplasticity means none of this is fixed: connections strengthen with use and weaken without it, throughout life.

Sensation, Perception and Consciousness

Top-down and bottom-up

Sensation is the raw intake of energy by the sense organs. Perception is the top-down way our brains organize and interpret that information and put it into context — which is why the same stimulus can be read two ways, and why expectation changes what is seen. Bottom-up processing builds upward from the data; top-down processing reaches downward from prior knowledge, and the two run together.

Consciousness is our awareness of ourselves and our environment. Attention is its bottleneck: selective attention means most of the available information never reaches awareness at all, as demonstrated by inattentional blindness.

Sleep and REM

Sleep runs in roughly 90-minute cycles through light sleep, deep slow-wave sleep and REM. Rapid Eye Movement is the recurring sleep stage during which vivid dreams commonly occur: the motor cortex is active but the brainstem is blocking those messages, so the muscles have the feel of paralysis — everything except the eyes. The arrangement means the dreamer does not act out the dream.

Deep sleep appears to matter most for consolidating memory and for physical repair, while REM proportion is highest in infancy. The whole cycle is timed by the circadian rhythm, an internal clock entrained by light.

Memory

The three stages

sensory memory → short-term (working) memory → long-term memory

Sensory memory holds an impression for a fraction of a second. Short-term or working memory holds only a handful of items for seconds at a time, and it is where thinking actually happens. Long-term memory is effectively unlimited in capacity, and includes procedural memory for skills. Getting material across the second boundary is the whole problem, and mnemonics and chunking are the standard tools — chunking works by packing several items into one unit so that limited capacity holds more.

Explicit and implicit

Explicit memory is willingly concentrated memory — facts and events you can deliberately bring to mind and state. Implicit memory is retention independent of conscious recollection: riding a bicycle, a conditioned flinch, the effect of an advertisement you do not remember seeing. The two rely on different brain systems, which is why amnesia can destroy one while leaving the other intact.

Strengthening and forgetting

Memory is made strong by recall. Retrieving something effortfully strengthens it far more than re-reading it does, which is why testing yourself beats highlighting. Spacing retrievals out over time works better still.

Encoding → storage → retrieval   (failure at any stage looks like "forgetting")
Ebbinghaus forgetting curve  →  steepest loss occurs soon after learning
Retrieval is reconstructive  →  each recall can alter the memory

Because recall rebuilds rather than replays, confident memories can be wrong — the basis of research on misinformation effects and eyewitness testimony.

Cognition and Language

Cognition is our thoughts, perspectives and expectations — all the mental activity of knowing, judging and deciding. Because that activity has to be fast, it runs largely on heuristics: mental shortcuts that are usually right and predictably wrong.

  • Availability — judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind
  • Representativeness — judging by resemblance to a stereotype rather than by base rates
  • Anchoring — being pulled toward the first number offered
  • Confirmation bias — seeking evidence that fits what is already believed
  • Framing — the same facts producing different choices depending on wording

Language is a set of spoken, written or signed words which combine together to communicate meaning. Its structure runs from phonemes to morphemes to grammar, and its productivity is what makes it distinct: a finite vocabulary and a finite set of rules generate an unlimited number of new sentences. Children acquire it on a striking schedule without formal teaching, which led Noam Chomsky to argue for an innate capacity, and there appears to be a critical period beyond which a first language cannot be fully acquired. Broca's area supports production and Wernicke's area comprehension, so damage to each produces a characteristically different aphasia.

Emotion and Motivation

Theories of emotion

The Schachter–Singer two-factor theory states that to experience emotion one must be physically aroused and cognitively label that arousal. The label matters as much as the arousal: the same racing heart becomes fear or excitement depending on how the situation is read. Two earlier theories disagree about the ordering.

TheorySequence
James–LangeStimulus → bodily arousal → emotion is the reading of the body
Cannon–BardStimulus → arousal and emotion occur simultaneously and independently
Schachter–SingerStimulus → arousal + cognitive label → specific emotion

The two-dimensional model

Emotions can be mapped on two axes — valence from pleasant to unpleasant, and arousal from high to low — which places the familiar states in four quadrants:

pleasant / positive  →  relaxed (low arousal) · enthusiastic (high arousal)
unpleasant / negative  →  sad (low arousal) · angry (high arousal)

Motivation is treated similarly as a mix of biological drives and higher goals. Maslow's hierarchy arranges them from physiological needs through safety, belonging and esteem to self-actualisation — a useful ordering, though the evidence for a strict sequence is weak.

Personality

Id, ego and superego

In psychoanalytic theory the mind is divided three ways. The id is unconscious drive; the superego is internalised moral prohibition; and the ego gets what the unconscious energy wants while balancing it against the superego and the demands of reality.

Defense mechanisms

Defense mechanisms are the ego's protective method of reducing anxiety by unconsciously distorting reality, and they form part of our personality rather than sitting outside it.

MechanismWhat it does
RepressionPushes threatening material out of awareness
DenialRefuses to accept an external reality
ProjectionAttributes one's own unacceptable impulses to someone else
RationalisationSupplies an acceptable reason in place of the real one
DisplacementRedirects an impulse toward a safer target
RegressionRetreats to an earlier stage of functioning
SublimationChannels the impulse into socially valued activity

Traits and social context

The social cognitive perspective describes personality as the interaction between our traits and their social context — not the trait alone, and not the situation alone, but each shaping the other over time through what Albert Bandura called reciprocal determinism.

Trait approaches instead try to measure the stable dimensions themselves. The most replicated is the five-factor model:

openness · conscientiousness · extraversion · agreeableness · neuroticism

Psychological Disorders

Distress and dysfunction

Psychological disorders and mental illness seem to first cause distress, the subjective feeling that something is wrong, which then leads to dysfunction, the inability to work and carry on ordinary life. Diagnosis generally requires that a pattern be persistent, distressing and impairing rather than merely unusual — the criteria are catalogued in the DSM and ICD, which are revised as the evidence changes.

Anxiety and OCD

  • OCD (Obsessive Compulsive Disorder) — intrusive obsessions paired with compulsions performed to relieve them
  • Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) — persistent, unfocused worry not tied to any one object
  • Phobias — intense fear of a specific object or situation, out of proportion to the actual danger
  • Panic attacks — sudden episodes of overwhelming physical arousal and dread

Mood, trauma and addiction

Depressive and bipolar disorders involve sustained disturbance of mood itself — depression as persistent low mood and loss of interest, bipolar disorder as alternation between depression and mania. Trauma-related conditions such as PTSD follow overwhelming events and involve re-experiencing, avoidance and hyperarousal. Addiction involves compulsive use despite harm, with tolerance and withdrawal, and is closely tied to the dopamine reward system.

Schizophrenia and dissociation

Schizophrenia involves a break in the relationship between thought and reality: hallucinations, delusions and disorganised speech, alongside negative symptoms such as flattened affect and withdrawal. Dissociative disorders involve disruptions of memory, identity or continuity of experience. Personality disorders are enduring, inflexible patterns of relating that cause difficulty across many situations rather than in one.

Why disorders arise

Disorders are caused by both the learning perspective — conditioning, reinforcement and modelling, which explain particularly well how a phobia is acquired and maintained — and the biological perspective: genetics, brain structure and neurotransmitter function. The two combine in the diathesis–stress model, where an inherited vulnerability only becomes a disorder when enough environmental stress is applied.

Treatment

Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy involves a therapist using a range of techniques to help overcome troubles, gain insight and achieve personal growth, and it is the initial step in treating most disorders. The main families differ in where they look for the problem.

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) — identifies and changes distorted thought patterns and the behaviours that maintain them; the best-evidenced approach for anxiety and depression
  • Behaviour therapy — uses conditioning directly, as in graduated exposure for phobias
  • Psychodynamic therapy — seeks insight into unconscious conflict and its origins
  • Humanistic therapy — provides unconditional positive regard so the person can move toward growth
  • Group and family therapy — treats the relationships rather than only the individual

Biomedical treatments

Biomedical treatments aim to physiologically change the brain's electrochemical state, using psychotropic drugs, magnetic impulses or electrical currents. Transcranial magnetic stimulation delivers the magnetic version, and electroconvulsive therapy the electrical one — now given under anaesthetic and reserved largely for severe depression that has not responded to anything else.

Pharmacotherapy

Pharmacotherapy uses drugs to affect the mental state, and they fall into four categories.

CategoryUsed forActs mainly on
AntipsychoticsSchizophrenia, mania, psychosisDopamine transmission
AnxiolyticsAnxiety, panic, acute agitationGABA (benzodiazepines)
AntidepressantsDepression, anxiety, OCDSerotonin and norepinephrine
Mood stabilizersBipolar disorderLithium and anticonvulsants

For most conditions the evidence favours combining medication with therapy over either alone.

Branches of Psychology

Individual psychology

In individual psychology there are three branches: cognitive, which studies the mental processes themselves; personality, which studies stable individual differences; and clinical, which assesses and treats disorder. Developmental, educational, health and industrial-organisational psychology sit alongside them.

Social psychology

Social psychology focuses on the power of the situation and on how each of us influences one another under particular conditions. Its recurring finding is that circumstances predict behaviour better than character does — and that people systematically underestimate this, an error known as the fundamental attribution error.

  • Conformity — Asch showed people will endorse an obviously wrong answer when a group states it first
  • Obedience — Milgram showed ordinary people following instructions to inflict what they believed was harm
  • Bystander effect — the more witnesses present, the less likely any one intervenes
  • Groupthink — cohesive groups suppressing dissent in favour of consensus

A stereotype is an overgeneralized belief about a particular group of people. It becomes prejudice when it carries an emotional charge, and discrimination when it becomes action. Contact between groups on equal footing, working toward a shared goal, is the most reliable way to reduce it — cooperation is the key.

Nature and nurture together

A combination of biological factors — genetic, neurological and biochemical influences — together with environment and experience add up to how we behave. The question is not which of the two matters but how they interact: genes shape sensitivity to environments, environments switch genes on and off, and behaviour then selects further environments.

genes ↔ environment ↔ behaviour
