Life Sciences

Molecules combine to form compounds, and within living organisms, genetic information stored in DNA is transcribed into RNA and then translated into proteins, forming the molecular basis of all life. The diversity of life is broadly classified into five kingdoms, each with its own subdivisions. What follows works outward from there: how life began, the molecules that carry it, the five kingdoms in full, and the signalling systems that run a body.

Part I

Origins of Life

Where the first living thing could have come from, and what makes something alive at all.

How life began

  • Charles Darwin → life may have come from a warm little pond.
  • Primordial soup → amino acids, methane (too old — ruled out).
  • Archean Eon → life began.
  • Habitability boundary → Earth was hot, the atmosphere was hostile, meteors and asteroids hit Earth, the Moon was formed and the crust melted and formed again.
  • Abiogenesis → non-life became life; cool, deep seas, oceans, water existed.
  • Biosignature boundary → life started to evolve.

Base pairing

A pairs with T and G with C, through hydrogen bonds — a base pair.

  • T, C → pyrimidines; A, G → purines
  • 10 base pairs → 1 turn; π–π interaction
  • Major and minor grooves: base-pair-specific information vs. base-pair non-specific information

Genome and epigenome

cell → nucleus → genome & epigenome

GENOME → the complete set of DNA found: 2 copies of 23 DNA strands, coiled around histones; about 1000 segments → genes, which regulate protein production.

EPIGENOME → chemical tags; modifies the genome for when to turn a gene on and off — let loose or tightly wrapped, to read information.

(DNA + histone) coil → nucleosome → supercoiling → chromosome → chromatin

Chromatin is all the genetic material in the cell nucleus.

Back to top

From DNA to protein

DNA → heredity material. In a eukaryotic cell, the nucleus, chloroplast and mitochondrion carry DNA → genome.

organism → cell → nucleus → chromosomes → nucleosomes → histones

DNA has a double-helix structure with base pairs; DNA strand + histones → chromatin, and a lot of this is a chromosome.

Transcription

The process of using a DNA template to create a strand of RNA (messenger).

RNA polymerase: gtac / TAC → caug / AUG → pre-mRNA

  • Introns: sections of mRNA that do not code for proteins.
  • Exons: sections of mRNA which become expressed into protein.

Introns are broken down and exons are joined together → mature mRNA → ribosome, where the information is translated into protein.

Translation

The process of making a protein.

Part IV

Signalling and the Brain

How one cell tells another what to do — by post through the blood, or by text across a synapse.

Control of hormone release (three ways)

  1. Hormonal stimulation → cells produce their own hormones → stimulate other cells to produce hormones → released into the blood stream. (These have “tropic” in their name; stimulation in response to another.)
  2. Humoral → glucose in blood (the stimulus) tells the pancreas to release insulin.
  3. Neural stimulation → a neuron stimulates the cell receptor to release hormones to blood vessels.
Back to top

The neuron

Across the synaptic gap, neurotransmitters (messengers) are released after an action potential; they reach receptor sites, jump up (inhibit / exhibit) and return back — reuptake.

Neurotransmitters which bind to another cell tell it:

  • whether to send a signal (excite), or
  • to prohibit sending a signal (inhibit).

neurotransmitter → receptor → organ, tissue, gland etc.

The nervous system

  • Central nervous system → brain, spinal cord
  • Peripheral nervous system → nerves which spread in and out

Receptor → a region of tissue, or a molecule in the cell membrane, which responds specifically to a particular neurotransmitter, hormone, antigen etc.

Parasympathetic NS (rest and digest) Sympathetic NS (fight or flight)

Acetylcholine (ACh)

  • Nicotinic → CNS → memory, cognition
  • Muscarinic → PNS → skeletal & muscular; autonomic NS (especially parasympathetic)
How does memory work? We store it; the more we retrieve data from what is stored in the brain, the stronger the neuropath becomes. Therefore memory of that particular thing will be easy to retrieve the next time.
