Theology

Theology is the study of how people have understood the divine — what they took the ultimate to be, what they believed it asked of them, and how those answers were argued over, written down and passed on. What follows works through it by tradition, beginning with the perspective these notes are written from, then the Abrahamic faiths, the traditions of India and East Asia, and finally the positions that reject the question and the questions every tradition ends up facing.

Part I

Approach

Where these notes are written from, and how the subject is studied at all.

The perspective

Theology in my perspective is the study of how people throughout history have understood God. We ourselves are part of creation, and therefore a created mind cannot fully comprehend the Creator, but we can attempt to understand through reflection and study.

The oldest writings related to this tradition are the Dead Sea Scrolls, which show how the Hebrews interpreted their Creator. Found in caves near Qumran from 1947 onward and dating from roughly the third century BCE to the first century CE, they are the oldest surviving manuscripts of the Hebrew scriptures — the texts themselves are older, but these are the earliest copies we can read.

The limit stated above is itself a theological position, and an old one: that the finite cannot contain the infinite, so all theological language is approximate. Traditions that accept it tend to develop a negative theology, describing God by what must be denied rather than by what can be asserted.

How theology is studied

Theology is not the same as religious studies. Theology reasons from within a tradition, taking its sources seriously as sources; religious studies describes traditions from outside as historical and social facts. Both appear on this page, and it is worth knowing which one is being read.

Part II

The Abrahamic Tradition

Three faiths tracing themselves to Abraham, sharing a beginning and disagreeing about almost everything after it.

The biblical narrative

According to this narrative, God created the universe, a dynamic system of space, time and matter. Humanity began with Adam, whose fall was followed by generations leading to Noah and the great flood. Even after the flood there were further transgressions.

Later Abraham, descended from Terah in the line of Shem, was called by God to follow Him. Through Isaac and Jacob, whose twelve sons formed the tribes of Israel, the story continues. Joseph became the right hand of Pharaoh and saved his family during famine, though later generations fell into captivity. Through Moses the Exodus occurred, and the nation of Israel was formed, guided by judges, prophets and kings.

creation  →  fall  →  flood  →  call of Abraham  →  Exodus  →  nation  →  prophets

All these events were seen as preparing the way for the Messiah, Christ Jesus, believed by Christians to be the Word of God who became flesh, fulfilled prophecies, lived a perfect life, died to save humanity, and rose again.

The structure is worth noting independently of whether one accepts it: the narrative is arranged as promise and fulfilment, so each episode is read forward toward what follows. That reading is what Christianity added; Judaism holds the same scriptures without it, and the disagreement between the two turns largely on this point rather than on the texts themselves.

Judaism

Judaism is one of the oldest Abrahamic faiths and shares the early creation narrative and covenant traditions dating to around 2000 BCE. It centers on the covenant between the one God, often referred to as YHWH, and the descendants of Abraham. This covenant was formalized through Moses and the giving of the Torah at Sinai.

Central beliefs include the declaration of the Shema — "Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One" — and the observance of 613 mitzvot or commandments concerning ethics, ritual and justice.

Scripture

TextContains
Tanakhthe Torah (law), the Nevi'im (Prophets) and the Ketuvim (Writings)
Oral Torahpreserved in the Mishnah and expanded in the Talmud

Practice

Jewish life involves rituals such as circumcision, bar or bat mitzvah, marriage traditions and mourning practices. Weekly Shabbat observance and festivals such as Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Passover and Hanukkah shape religious life.

Branches and schools

Over time several branches developed, including Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist traditions, differing chiefly over how far the commandments bind and how they are interpreted in changed circumstances. Jewish thought also includes historical schools such as the teachings associated with Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel — two rival houses of interpretation whose recorded disagreements became a model for how argument itself could be preserved rather than resolved.

Christianity

After his ascension, his followers spread this message and many became martyrs. They formed the early centers of Christianity in Jerusalem, Antioch, Alexandria, Constantinople and Rome. Over time various interpretations and denominations emerged as people developed different theological and social understandings of the church.

Scripture and creed

The Christian Bible holds the Hebrew scriptures as the Old Testament alongside a New Testament of four gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the epistles and Revelation. Its central claims were fixed in creeds by councils of bishops when disputes could not be settled otherwise: Nicaea in 325 on the divinity of Christ, and Chalcedon in 451 on how divine and human natures are held together in one person.

Divisions

WhenDivision
451 onward Churches rejecting Chalcedon separate — the Oriental Orthodox communion
1054 The Great Schism divides Rome from Constantinople: Catholic and Eastern Orthodox
1517 onward The Reformation produces the Protestant churches — Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and later Baptist, Methodist and Pentecostal

The recurring points of disagreement are consistent across all three splits: where authority lies — in scripture alone, in scripture read through tradition, or in a teaching office — how salvation is received, and what the sacraments do.

Four of the five early centres named above — Jerusalem, Antioch, Alexandria and Constantinople — lie in the eastern Mediterranean. Christianity's centre of gravity moved west later, and in the last century has moved substantially to Africa, Asia and Latin America.

Islam

Islam originated in seventh-century Arabia with the Prophet Muhammad, who is believed to have received revelations from God through the angel Gabriel. These revelations were compiled in the Quran, considered the final and preserved scripture in Islam. The life and sayings of Muhammad recorded in hadith literature serve as guidance alongside the Quran.

Belief

Central to Islamic belief is Tawhid, the absolute oneness of God, along with belief in angels, earlier scriptures, prophets from Adam to Muhammad, the Day of Judgment, and divine destiny.

The Five Pillars

Division, law and mysticism

After Muhammad's death in 632 CE, the community divided over leadership, leading to the Sunni and Shia traditions. Islamic law, known as Sharia, guides ethical and social life, developed through schools of jurisprudence that read the Quran and hadith alongside analogy and scholarly consensus. Mystical traditions such as Sufism emphasize spiritual experience and closeness to the divine.

Throughout history the Islamic world contributed greatly to science, medicine, mathematics and philosophy during the Islamic Golden Age — the period in which Greek philosophy was preserved, translated and argued with, and in which Aristotle reached medieval Europe by way of Arabic commentary.

Part III

Traditions of India and East Asia

Traditions organised less around a covenant than around a way — of release, of harmony, or of conduct.

Hinduism

Hinduism developed over thousands of years in the Indian subcontinent and does not have a single founder. It encompasses a wide range of philosophies, rituals and traditions — which is why it resists being described as one system with one doctrine.

Central ideas

TermMeaning
Brahmanthe ultimate reality
Atmanthe individual soul
Karmathe law of action and consequence
Samsarathe cycle of rebirth
Mokshaliberation from that cycle

Hindu thought includes many deities and traditions, such as the Trimurti of Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, as well as various forms of the divine feminine. Schools differ on how Atman and Brahman are related: whether the two are ultimately identical, distinct, or somehow both.

Texts and paths

Sacred texts include the Vedas, the Upanishads, the epics Mahabharata and Ramayana, and later Puranic literature. Paths to spiritual growth include knowledge, devotion, action and meditation — several routes rather than one, suited to different temperaments.

jnana (knowledge)  •  bhakti (devotion)  •  karma (action)  •  dhyana (meditation)

Religious practice often involves rituals known as puja, temple worship, pilgrimages and festivals such as Diwali and Holi. Hindu traditions have also influenced philosophical and ethical ideas such as nonviolence, which shaped figures like Mahatma Gandhi.

Buddhism

Buddhism began in the fifth century BCE with Siddhartha Gautama, who later became known as the Buddha or "the Enlightened One." After encountering suffering in the world, he taught a path aimed at understanding and overcoming it.

The Four Noble Truths

Truth
1There is suffering
2It originates in craving
3It can cease
4There is a path to its end — the Noble Eightfold Path

Buddhism also teaches the impermanence of all things, the absence of a permanent self, and the importance of wisdom, ethical conduct and meditation. The goal is nirvana, liberation from the cycle of rebirth.

The denial of a permanent self is the sharpest break with the Hindu traditions it emerged alongside: rebirth is retained, but there is no unchanging Atman that carries across it — only a continuing process.

Traditions

Different traditions developed over time, including Theravada in Southeast Asia, Mahayana in East Asia, and Vajrayana in Tibet. Buddhist communities are centered around the Sangha, the monastic and lay community that preserves and practices the teachings.

Daoism

Daoism emerged in ancient China and is associated with thinkers such as Laozi and Zhuangzi. The central idea is the Dao, the fundamental way or principle underlying the universe. Daoist teachings encourage harmony with natural patterns rather than forcing outcomes.

Concepts such as yin and yang describe the dynamic balance of opposites, while qi represents the vital energy that flows through all things. Practices include meditation, philosophy, martial arts and traditional healing methods, all aimed at living in harmony with the natural order.

The way that can be spoken of is not the constant way.

The opening of the Daodejing makes the same move as the negative theologies of the Abrahamic traditions, from the opposite direction: the ultimate is not withheld because it is too holy to name, but because naming is the wrong kind of act toward it.

Confucianism

Confucianism began with the teachings of Confucius in ancient China around the sixth century BCE. It focuses less on theology and more on ethical and social harmony. Important values include filial piety, humaneness and proper conduct within society.

Confucius taught that a stable and moral society depends on respect, education and the cultivation of virtue. His teachings were later compiled in the Analects and became deeply influential in East Asian philosophy and governance.

Whether Confucianism is a religion at all is genuinely contested. It has ritual, canonical texts and an account of the ultimate order of things, but it makes few claims about gods and little about an afterlife — which is why it appears on some lists of world religions and not others.

Shinto

Shinto is the indigenous spiritual tradition of Japan and is often described as "the way of the kami," referring to spiritual beings or forces present in nature and in ancestors. It has no single founder or central scripture, though early myths appear in texts such as the Kojiki and Nihongi.

According to these myths, the cosmos emerged from primordial chaos and the first heavenly deities appeared, followed by generations of divine beings including Izanagi and Izanami who created the islands of Japan and many other kami.

Nature itself is considered sacred, and humans live alongside these spirits. Ritual purification practices and shrine worship are central to Shinto life. Kami are believed to inhabit natural places and sacred objects, and harmony with them is maintained through respect, offerings and rituals performed by shrine priests.

Because Shinto makes few exclusive demands, most Japanese practice sits alongside Buddhism rather than in competition with it — commonly with Shinto rites for birth and marriage and Buddhist ones for death.

Part IV

Other Traditions and Positions

Traditions carried across oceans, the position that rejects the whole question, and what the wider record shows.

African diasporic religions

African diasporic religions developed from indigenous African traditions that spread through the transatlantic slave trade and later blended with elements of Christianity and Islam. Traditions such as Vodou in Haiti, Santería in Cuba and Candomblé in Brazil reflect this blending.

Many of these traditions view the universe as interconnected through a spiritual realm, the human realm and the ancestral realm.

spiritual realm  •  human realm  •  ancestral realm

Deities and spirits are honored through rituals, music, dance, offerings and festivals. Ancestors play an important role, and community identity is often preserved through these spiritual practices. These traditions emphasize harmony between people, nature and the spiritual world.

The blending was frequently a survival strategy rather than a theological choice: practice forbidden outright could continue under the cover of permitted saints and feast days, and the correspondences that resulted became part of the traditions themselves.

Atheism and humanism

Atheism is not a religion but a philosophical position that rejects belief in gods or supernatural beings. It emphasizes evidence, reason and human-centered ethics. Although skepticism toward gods appears in ancient philosophy, atheism became more prominent during the Enlightenment and with the growth of modern science.

Some forms of atheism actively deny the existence of deities, while others simply lack belief. The distinction matters, because the two carry different burdens of argument.

PositionClaim
Strong atheismasserts that no deity exists
Weak atheismsimply lacks the belief, without asserting the denial
Agnosticismholds the question unresolved, or unresolvable
Ignosticismholds the question insufficiently defined to answer

Many atheists adopt ethical frameworks based on humanism, emphasizing compassion, human rights and rational inquiry rather than divine authority. The standing challenge to that project is the one raised in the Euthyphro dilemma from the other direction: whether a morality with no external anchor can bind, and whether it needs to.

Other beliefs and observations

Throughout history many cultures practiced polytheism, believing in multiple gods associated with natural forces. Monuments such as Stonehenge or ancient Egyptian sun worship reflect early attempts to understand cosmic order. Some traditions developed ideas of a single supreme force governing the universe, while others focused on interactions between the human and spirit worlds.

Recurring forms

Polytheismmany gods exist, often tied to natural forces
Monotheismthere is exactly one God
Henotheismmany gods exist but one is worshipped
PantheismGod and the universe are identical

The brain at prayer

Modern scientific studies have even examined how the brain responds during meditation or prayer, suggesting that belief and faith can influence neurological activity. Contemplative practice shows measurable effects on attention networks and on stress response, and long-term practitioners show differences in the regions involved.

What such findings can and cannot establish is worth being precise about. That a practice changes the brain is a claim about the practice, not about whether its object exists — the same measurements would be expected either way.

These observations show that humanity's search for meaning and understanding of the divine has taken many different forms across cultures and time.

Part V

Questions Across Traditions

The arguments that recur wherever the subject is taken up, and how the traditions line up against each other.

Arguments about God

Natural theology asks what could be established without appeal to revelation. Four classical arguments recur, each with a standing objection — and none of them is regarded as settled by everyone.

ArgumentRunsObjection
Cosmological everything that begins has a cause; the universe began; so it has a cause why must the cause be exempt from the rule, and why call it God?
Teleological the order and fine-tuning of the world suggests design order can arise from undirected processes; apparent tuning may be selection effect
Ontological a greatest conceivable being would have to exist, or it would not be greatest existence may not be a property that can be reasoned into being
Moral binding moral obligation requires a source beyond human preference obligation may be grounded in reason, contract or sentiment instead

Notably, several traditions on this page are indifferent to the whole exercise. The question "does God exist" is central to the Abrahamic faiths, marginal in Confucianism, and reframed entirely in Buddhism, where the pressing question is not what exists but how suffering ends.

The problem of evil

The hardest problem for monotheism is stated in three lines that seem individually plausible and jointly impossible.

God is all-powerful. God is perfectly good. Evil exists.

Responses — theodicies — deny that the three actually conflict.

ResponseSays
Free will defencegenuine freedom entails the possibility of its misuse; the freedom is worth the cost
Soul-makinga world with hardship develops virtues a frictionless one could not
Limited perspectivea created mind may be unable to see how a particular evil fits — the position stated at the top of this page
Reframingsome traditions relocate the problem: karma distributes consequence across lives, and dualist systems posit an opposing principle

The version hardest to answer is not moral evil but natural evil — earthquake, disease, the suffering of children and animals — because no appeal to human freedom reaches it.

Read across the rows, one division does most of the work. Where the ultimate is a person who acts and can be addressed, the tradition organises itself around covenant, obedience and relationship. Where the ultimate is a principle or a state of affairs, it organises itself around alignment, insight and practice. The Abrahamic faiths sit on one side of that line and most of Part III on the other — which is why the two groups disagree less often than they simply ask different questions.

A caution on tables like this one: every cell compresses centuries of internal disagreement into a phrase, and practitioners of any row would qualify their own. It is a map, and no map is the ground.
