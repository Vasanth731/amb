Economics

Resources are limited and every resource can be used in more than one way, so choices have to be made — and economics is the study of those choices. What follows works outward from that single problem: where trade came from, how one market sets a price, how millions of markets add up to a national economy, and where commerce, finance and the stock market sit inside all of it, down to the five numbers a market publishes each day.

Part I

Foundations

Where exchange came from, what the subject actually studies, and how it divides itself up.

How exchange became an economy

Each stage below produced the surplus that made the next one possible, so the order carries the argument.

  • Barter → food for food. Early humans moved with their food supply and traded goods directly — which only works when two people happen to want exactly what the other has spare.
  • Agricultural revolution → farms, villages, tools → the first surplus. Food beyond what the household could eat can be stored, traded, taxed, or used to feed someone doing a different job.
  • Shipment → surplus travels → exchange outgrows the village. Money emerges as a common measure of value, so trade no longer needs a coincidence of wants.
  • Industrial revolution → machines → output per worker multiplies. Factories, invested capital, wage labour and the modern firm all arrive here.
  • Information age → services and information dominate output; capital crosses borders in seconds; markets price expectations about the future rather than only goods in the present.

barter → money → credit → digital payment

Specialisation is the hinge. Nobody can be a farmer, weaver, smith and trader at once, so the moment surplus allows people to do one thing well, everything else — trade, money, firms, cities — follows almost inevitably.

What economics studies

Economics is the study of the use of scarce resources that have alternative uses.

Both halves of that matter. Resources are limited, and any resource has alternative uses — so using it here means not using it there.

humans → unlimited desires   •   resources → limited, alternative uses

→ we need to make choices → economists study those choices

Every choice carries both a benefit and a cost. The cost is not only the money paid, but the value of the next best thing given up — opportunity cost.

Part II

Microeconomics

Individual agents and single markets: how one price gets set, how a firm decides, and when the whole mechanism fails.

Supply, demand and price

  • Demand → quantity buyers want → falls as price rises.
  • Supply → quantity sellers offer → rises as price rises.
  • Equilibrium → where the two meet → the market price.

Price as a signal

A price compresses the scarcity of a thing and the desire for it into one number that everybody can act on. It tells producers what is worth making and buyers what they must give up. This is how a market economy coordinates millions of unrelated decisions with no central planner.

Elasticity

How sharply quantity responds to a change in price or income.

  • Inelastic → salt, medicine → people buy about the same amount whatever it costs.
  • Elastic → restaurant meals, air travel → demand collapses when the price rises.

Elasticity decides who actually bears a tax: whichever side is less able to walk away pays most of it.

Back to top

Choice at the margin

scarcity → choice → opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost → the value of the next best alternative given up. An hour of study costs whatever that hour could otherwise have produced.
  • Marginal thinking → decisions happen at the edge. What matters is the benefit and cost of the next unit, not the average over all of them.
  • Utility → satisfaction from consuming something. Diminishing marginal utility → the second slice of cake is worth less than the first, the tenth much less again — which is why demand curves slope downward.
  • Sunk cost → money already spent and unrecoverable. It should not affect the next decision, although it almost always does.
  • Incentives → people respond to payoffs, sometimes in ways the rule-maker never intended. Most economic surprises are incentive surprises.

Firms, costs and competition

Costs

  • Fixed cost → does not change with output → rent, machinery.
  • Variable cost → changes with output → materials, power.
  • Marginal cost → the cost of producing one more unit.
  • Economies of scale → average cost falls as output grows, because fixed costs spread over more units — a large part of why big firms exist at all.

revenue − cost → profit   •   produce while marginal revenue > marginal cost

Market structures

StructureSellersPricing powerExample
Perfect competition very many none — price taker vegetable market
Monopolistic competition many, differentiated a little, through branding restaurants, salons
Oligopoly a few large substantial, interdependent telecom, airlines
Monopoly one sets the price railways, utilities

Competition is the pressure that pushes price toward cost and forces firms to improve. Structure therefore decides not just price but how much innovation happens.

Inside the organisation

Commerce is the activity of buying and selling on a large scale — the conduct of trade among economic agents. Business and management studies is concerned with the way a company or organisation operates and functions.

Economics asks a prior question: why do firms exist at all, rather than everyone contracting individually for each task? The answer is transaction costs — negotiating, measuring and enforcing every exchange is expensive, and often cheaper to do inside an organisation than in the open market.

What an organisation actually combines

InputMeaning
Knowledgewhat is known in a particular field; learned through education
Skilla particular ability to do something, arising from talent, training and practice
Toolsa device used to carry out a particular function
Techniquesa way of carrying out a particular task

knowledge, skills, tools, techniques → applied to activities → requirements met

That is also the definition of project management: the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities in order to meet project requirements. In economic terms it is the management of labour and capital at the level of a single task.

Labour and wages

  • A wage is the price of labour, set in a market like any other price.
  • Human capital → knowledge and ability built through education, training and practice → the main reason wages rise over a career.
  • Productivity → output per worker → what makes higher wages affordable rather than merely demanded.

When markets fail

Markets misallocate when the price misses something real.

FailureWhat goes wrongExample
Externality costs or benefits fall on people outside the transaction factory smoke; vaccination
Public good cannot easily be sold to one person at a time → free riders street lighting, defence
Information asymmetry one side knows much more than the other used cars, insurance
Monopoly power output restricted, price raised above cost a sole supplier

These are the standard economic arguments for taxes, subsidies and regulation — each is an attempt to put the missing cost or benefit back into the price.

Market failure is an argument for intervention, not proof that intervention will work. Governments face the same information problems, and that symmetry is the whole of the policy debate.

Part III

Macroeconomics

The whole economy as one system: what it produces, why prices and jobs move, and the two levers used on it.

Measuring the whole economy

Gross domestic product → the total market value of final goods and services produced in a country over a period.

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

  • C → household consumption
  • I → investment by firms
  • G → government spending
  • X − M → exports minus imports
  • Nominal GDP → at current prices.
  • Real GDP → inflation removed → the one to use for comparisons over time.
  • GDP per capita → divided by population → closer to a measure of living standards.

The circular flow of income

GDP counts what is bought and sold. Unpaid housework, volunteering and leisure are missing; pollution and traffic accidents can push it up. It is a measure of activity, not of wellbeing.

Inflation and unemployment

Inflation

A general rise in the price level, so each unit of money buys less. Measured with index numbers — the consumer price index tracks a fixed basket of goods over time.

  • Demand-pull → too much spending chasing too few goods.
  • Cost-push → input costs rise → fuel, wages.
  • Deflation → prices falling → sounds pleasant, usually worse: people postpone spending, so output falls further.

nominal return − inflation → real return

Unemployment

TypeCauseRemedy
Frictionalthe gap between leaving one job and finding anotherbetter job matching
Structuralskills do not match the jobs that existtraining, retraining
Cyclicalweak demand during a downturnmonetary or fiscal support
Seasonalwork that only exists part of the yearlittle — expected

The labour force counts only people working or actively looking. Someone who stops looking leaves the statistic entirely, which is why the unemployment rate can fall for a bad reason.

The business cycle

Output does not grow smoothly. It moves through a repeating but irregular sequence.

expansion → peak → contraction → trough → expansion

  • Expansion → output, employment and confidence rising.
  • Peak → capacity strained; inflation usually building.
  • Contraction → spending falls, firms cut output and jobs. A sustained contraction is a recession; a severe, prolonged one a depression.
  • Trough → the low point, from which recovery begins.

The cycle is largely self-reinforcing: falling demand cuts incomes, which cuts demand again. Breaking that feedback loop is the main practical argument for macroeconomic policy.

Money and banking

What money does

  • Medium of exchange → removes the need for a coincidence of wants.
  • Unit of account → a common measure so prices can be compared.
  • Store of value → purchasing power carried into the future.

How banks create money

deposit → bank lends most of it → the loan becomes another deposit → repeat

Most of the money supply is not printed by anyone — it is created by commercial banks lending. This is also why banking is fragile: the deposits are promised on demand while the loans are long term, so a loss of confidence alone can break a solvent bank.

Monetary and fiscal policy

Monetary policyFiscal policy
Whothe central bank (RBI, Federal Reserve)the government
Toolspolicy interest rate, money supply, reserve requirementstaxation, spending, borrowing
Aimprice stability, then growthdemand, distribution, public services
Speedfast to decide, slow to actslow to decide, fast to act

The interest rate is the price of money over time, and it is arguably the most consequential single number in an economy. Raising it makes borrowing dearer, cools spending, and lowers the present value of every future cash flow — which is why nearly all asset prices fall together when rates rise.

  • Deficit → spending exceeds revenue in a year.
  • Public debt → deficits accumulated over many years.
  • Multiplier → government spending becomes somebody's income, which is spent again → the total effect can exceed the original amount.

Trade between nations

Comparative advantage → produce whatever you give up least to make, and trade for the rest — even if the other country is better at everything. Specialisation still leaves both sides with more.

TermMeaning
Exports (X)goods and services sold abroad
Imports (M)goods and services bought from abroad
Tariffa tax on imports
Quotaa limit on the quantity imported
Balance of paymentsthe record of all transactions with the rest of the world
Exchange ratethe price of one currency in another

currency weakens → exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home

Trade raises total output but not everyone's income. The gains are spread thinly across consumers while the losses concentrate in specific industries and towns — which is why the politics of trade is so much harder than the economics of it.

Part IV

Commerce, Finance and Markets

Where trade, money over time, shares and the stock market sit inside the subject — and how they join the two levels together.

Trade and commerce

  • Trade → the voluntary exchange of goods or services between economic agents. Both sides expect to gain, or neither would agree.
  • Commerce → buying and selling at large scale, plus everything that makes routine exchange possible: transport, warehousing, insurance, advertising, payment. Commerce is trade plus its infrastructure.
  • Specialisation → the reason trade exists at all, whether between two people or two countries.

This is the cleanest bridge between the two halves of the subject: comparative advantage explains both why a person picks an occupation (micro) and why nations trade (macro), using exactly the same argument.

Back to top

Finance

Finance deals with how money is raised, allocated and managed over time, when the future is uncertain. It sits inside economics and operates at both levels at once.

DomainConcernsLevel
Personal financehousehold saving, borrowing, insurancemicro
Corporate financehow a firm raises money and which projects it fundsmicro
Public financegovernment revenue and expendituremacro — this is fiscal policy

Core ideas

  • Time value of money → a rupee today beats a rupee next year, because today's rupee can be put to work. Discounting runs that backwards to value future amounts in today's terms.
  • Compounding → returns themselves earn returns, so growth accelerates the longer it is left alone. The same mathematics as economic growth, applied to one balance.
  • Risk and return → higher expected return demands more uncertainty. Nothing offers high return at low risk for long, because others would buy it until the price rose.
  • Diversification → spreading money across assets that do not move together lowers risk without giving up the same proportion of return.

The financial system

household savings → banks, funds, markets → firm investment → output → income

Banks, insurers, mutual funds and pension funds channel savings toward whoever wants to invest. That is finance's macroeconomic job — and when the channel breaks, as in a banking crisis, output and employment fall even though nothing is wrong with the factories themselves.

Financial markets are institutions inside finance, and they are where ownership of productive assets is priced and exchanged.

Primary and secondary markets

company issues new shares → primary market → the company receives the money

investor sells to investor → secondary market → the company receives nothing

An initial public offering is a primary-market event. Almost all daily trading is secondary — and only the primary market actually funds production, which is worth remembering whenever the stock market is described as if it were the economy.

What markets signal

  • Capital allocation → money flows toward the firms investors believe can use it best. The same job prices do everywhere else, applied to capital.
  • Leading indicator → a share price reflects expected future profits, so markets tend to turn before the real economy does.
  • Wealth effect → rising markets make households feel richer, so they spend more — a market movement becoming a macroeconomic one.
  • Index → NIFTY 50, Sensex, S&P 500 → a basket of shares tracked as one number, used as a benchmark and a barometer.

Investment, speculation, bubbles

  • Investment → buying an asset for the income and growth it produces.
  • Speculation → buying it expecting to resell higher.

Both are necessary, since speculators supply the liquidity that lets everyone else trade. But when enough buyers hold an asset only because it has been rising, the price detaches from anything the business will ever earn. That is a bubble, and its collapse is a macroeconomic event rather than a private misfortune.

Prices aggregate what people believe, not what is true. That makes markets an extraordinary information system and an unreliable oracle at the same time — and both halves of that sentence matter.
Back to top

Measuring and modelling

Econometrics → statistics applied to real economic data, to test whether the theories hold and to measure how large an effect is rather than only its direction.

  • Model → a deliberate simplification, kept only while it predicts better than the alternatives.
  • Index number → many prices or quantities compressed into one comparable series → CPI, GDP deflator, a stock index.
  • Correlation is not causation → two series moving together may share a hidden cause, or the causation may run the other way.
  • Ceteris paribus → “all else equal” → the assumption that lets one variable be examined at a time, and the reason predictions fail when everything moves at once.

This is where economics stops being argument and starts being calculation on real data — and where most of the honest disagreement in the subject now lives.

Part V

Financial Economics

The working layer: what is traded, how an order reaches the market, and the numbers a market actually produces.

The four branches

Financial economics studies how money, risk and time are priced. It divides into four areas that build on each other.

  • Financial markets → what gets traded
    • stocks, bonds, commodities, currencies
  • Investments → what you hold and why
    • shares, portfolio, diversification, exchange-traded funds
  • Trading → how a holding is entered and exited
    • buy and sell, long and short, bid and ask, orders, liquidity
  • Quantitative finance → how any of it is measured
    • returns, volatility, Sharpe ratio, drawdown, risk, algorithmic trading

Part IV placed these inside economics. This part goes down one level, to the mechanics and the measurements.

Stock, share and market cap

The two words are used interchangeably, with a slight distinction.

  • Stock → ownership in companies as a general concept → “I invest in stocks.”
  • Share → one specific unit of that ownership → “I own 100 shares of Company X.”

stock = the security in general   •   share = one unit of it

Market capitalisation

Roughly what the market thinks the whole equity of a company is worth.

market cap = share price × shares outstanding

Worked example
Share price₹500
Shares outstanding10 crore
Market cap₹500 × 10 crore = ₹5,000 crore

This is why companies are grouped as large-cap, mid-cap and small-cap. The tiers matter less for prestige than for liquidity and volatility: smaller companies are harder to buy and sell in quantity, and their prices move further.

Market price is not company value

Suppose you judge a company to be worth ₹1,000 per share, but investors are pessimistic and it trades at ₹700. You consider it undervalued and buy. If the market eventually agrees and the price reaches ₹1,000, you profit. That is the whole idea behind fundamental investing.

Market cap tells you what the market currently says a company is worth, not what it is worth. Keeping those two apart is the difference between investing on a judgement and investing on a quotation.

Why prices move

At the simplest level, price moves because buyers and sellers interact. Suppose a stock trades at ₹100.

many buyers, few sellers → ₹101 → ₹102 → ₹103 → price rises

many sellers, few buyers → ₹99 → ₹98 → ₹97 → price falls

This is the supply and demand of Part II, applied to a security instead of a vegetable. The harder question is why people suddenly want to buy or sell.

  • The company → earnings, revenue, profit, debt, management, orders won or lost
  • The economy → growth, interest rates, inflation, government policy, taxes
  • The industry → how competitors are performing, input prices, regulation
  • The world → global events, oil prices, foreign investor flows, currency moves
  • The mood → expectations and sentiment, which can move a price with no news at all
Prices respond to changes in expectations, not to good news as such. A company can report record profit and fall the same morning, because the profit was smaller than what the price already assumed. This is the single most confusing feature of markets for a newcomer, and the most important one.

Exchange, broker and the order route

A stock exchange is where securities are traded. In India the major ones are the NSE (National Stock Exchange) and the BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange). A company lists its shares on an exchange.

You do not walk onto the exchange yourself.

you → broker → exchange → matched with a buyer or seller

What a broker does

  • accepts your order and sends it to the exchange
  • maintains your trading and demat account
  • provides market data and price quotes
  • executes trades and reports them back to you
  • collects the exchange fees, taxes and its own brokerage

After the trade

Agreeing a trade and completing it are separate events. Clearing works out who owes what; settlement is the actual transfer of shares and money, guaranteed by a clearing corporation so neither side needs to trust the other. Indian equities completed the move to T+1 settlement in 2023 — shares and cash change hands one working day after the trade.

Bid, ask and order types

At any instant there is a highest price a buyer will pay and a lowest price a seller will accept.

Bid₹99the highest price someone is currently willing to pay
Ask₹100the lowest price someone is currently willing to sell for
Spread₹1ask − bid

The spread is a real cost, not a quirk of notation: you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so a round trip loses the spread before the price has moved at all. All the unfilled bids and asks together form the order book.

  • Slippage → the gap between the price you expected and the price you got.

Long and short

Long

buy → price rises → sell

Buy at ₹100, sell at ₹120 → profit ₹20 per share. You own the shares, and the most you can lose is what you paid.

Short

borrow and sell → price falls → buy back and return

Sell at ₹100, buy back at ₹80 → profit ₹20 per share. You profit when the price falls, which lets a position be taken against a company rather than only for it.

The two are not mirror images. A long position can lose at most 100%, because a price stops at zero. A short position has no such limit — the price can keep rising — and the borrowed shares must be returned whether or not you were eventually right.

Open, high, low, close, volume

A market does not publish a single price. For each period — usually one trading day — it publishes five numbers that summarise everything that happened. Four prices and one quantity.

The five standard parameters
ParameterMeaningWhat it tells you
Open price of the first trade of the period where the market resumed after the overnight gap
High highest price paid during the period the furthest buyers were willing to chase
Low lowest price paid during the period the furthest sellers were willing to give up
Close price of the last trade of the period the day's settled verdict — used for valuation and reporting
Volume number of shares traded in total activity and conviction — not a price at all

On the NSE and BSE the period runs from 9:15 to 15:30 IST, so the open and close are the first and last trades within that window. These five numbers are the smallest summary that still shows the shape of a period: where it started, where it ended, and how far it wandered in between.

Numbers derived from the five

ParameterDefinition
Rangehigh − low → how far the period travelled
Bodyclose − open → the net movement
Gaptoday's open − yesterday's close → what happened while the market was shut
Return(today's close − yesterday's close) ÷ yesterday's close
Turnovervolume × price → money traded, not shares traded
VWAPvolume-weighted average price → the average price actually paid
52-week high / lowthe extremes of the past year → a common reference level

Two adjustments that matter

  • Adjusted close → the closing price rewritten to remove stock splits and dividends. A 1:5 split turns one ₹2,000 share into five ₹400 shares — nothing of value changed, but the raw price shows an 80% crash. Without adjustment, every split looks like a catastrophe.
  • Delivery volume → the part of the day's volume where shares actually changed hands, rather than being bought and sold again the same day. Indian exchanges report it separately, and it separates genuine accumulation from intraday churn.

Two more parameters appear once you leave plain shares: open interest, the number of derivative contracts still outstanding, and circuit limits or price bands, the exchange-set range beyond which a stock cannot move in a session.

Only these five are observations. Everything else on this page — returns, volatility, averages, indicators — is something somebody calculated from them, which means each is also a place where an assumption can quietly enter.

A portfolio is simply the collection of assets held.

Diversification

Rather than ₹1,00,000 into one company, spread it — ₹25,000 each into four. The point is not to improve the expected return but to stop depending on any single outcome, which removes company-specific risk.

diversification removes company-specific risk → it cannot remove market risk

What it does not do is protect you from a market-wide fall, and it only works to the extent the holdings differ. Four software companies are one bet written four times. The relevant measure is correlation — how closely two holdings move together — and its most awkward property is that correlations rise toward one in a crisis, exactly when the protection was needed.

Funds

  • Mutual fund → pooled money managed on your behalf, priced once a day.
  • Index fund → holds a whole index mechanically, at low cost.
  • ETF → an exchange-traded fund → the same idea, but bought and sold on the exchange like a share.

An index cannot be bought — it is a calculation. A fund tracking it can be, which is how “buying the market” is done in practice.

Quantitative finance

Return

return = (final − initial) ÷ initial

  • ₹100 → ₹110  →  (110 − 100) ÷ 100 = 0.10 → +10%
  • ₹100 → ₹90  →  (90 − 100) ÷ 100 = −0.10 → −10%

Profit versus return

InvestedProfitReturn
Person A₹1,000₹10010%
Person B₹1,00,000₹1,0001%

B made ten times the money; A made the better decision. Percentage returns compare skill, absolute profit compares size, and confusing the two is how large portfolios get credited with performance they never achieved.

Volatility

  • Stock A → 100, 101, 100, 102, 101 → low volatility
  • Stock B → 100, 120, 90, 130, 80 → high volatility

Measured as the standard deviation of returns, usually annualised by multiplying by the square root of the number of trading periods in a year. Volatility describes the size of movement, never its direction: a highly volatile stock is not more likely to rise.

Risk-adjusted measures

MeasureWhat it asks
Sharpe ratioreturn per unit of volatility → was the gain worth the ride?
Sortino ratiothe same, counting only downside movement as risk
Drawdownhow far below the previous peak you currently are
Maximum drawdownthe worst such fall over the whole period → the pain that had to be sat through

Drawdown is the number that decides whether a strategy is survivable rather than merely profitable. A 60% fall needs a 150% gain just to get back to where it started.

Kinds of risk

Risk is the possibility that the outcome differs from what was expected, particularly for the worse. An expected +10% might in fact deliver +30%, +10%, −5% or −40%; that uncertainty is the risk.

RiskThe thing that goes wrong
Marketthe whole market falls → cannot be diversified away
Company-specificone firm fails → can be diversified away
Liquidityyou cannot sell without moving the price against yourself
Credita borrower does not repay
Interest-raterates move and reprice everything you hold
Currencythe exchange rate moves against a foreign holding

Algorithmic trading

Rules stated precisely enough for a computer to execute them, tested against historical data before being used. It is quantitative finance applied, and it lives or dies on the costs most descriptions leave out: brokerage, exchange fees, securities transaction tax and stamp duty, the bid-ask spread, and the impact of one's own order.

Costs are charged on every transaction, while an edge is earned only on average. That asymmetry is why a strategy that trades ten times more often needs to be far more than ten times better.

How the two levels connect

Macroeconomics is not a separate subject from microeconomics; it is what you get when millions of individual choices are added together. A household deciding to save rather than spend is a micro decision. All households deciding it at once is a fall in aggregate demand, and a recession.

The same idea at two scales
MicroMacro
a price in one marketthe general price level → inflation
one firm's cost of capitalthe central bank's policy rate
a household choosing to savethe national savings rate
one worker between jobsthe unemployment rate
